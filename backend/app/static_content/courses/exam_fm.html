Part I — Time Value of Money (5–15%)

Every other topic on this exam is built on top of the ideas in this part, even though it carries the smallest exam weight of any topic. Here you will learn how money grows over time under simple and compound interest, how the many equivalent ways of quoting an interest rate (nominal, effective, discount, force of interest) relate to one another, and how to compare cash flows that occur at different points in time using an equation of value. Master the vocabulary and the conversion mechanics in these four chapters and the annuity, loan, and bond material later in the manual will feel like straightforward extensions rather than new subjects.

1. Simple & Compound Interest

Learning Outcomes Covered: Define interest, simple and compound growth, the accumulation function, present and future value, net present value, the discount factor, and the connection between inflation and the real rate of return; be able to solve for any one of rate, time, present value, or future value when the other three are known.

Interest as Rent Paid on Money

Interest is the compensation a borrower pays a lender for the use of the lender's money over time — think of it as rent charged on cash instead of on property. Money has a "time value" precisely because a dollar available today can be put to work (invested, lent, spent to avoid a future higher price) in a way that a dollar promised a year from now cannot. If you deposit $1 into an account today, the account's growth over the next t years is tracked by a function called the accumulation function, written a(t), with a(0) = 1 by convention. Multiplying any starting principal by a(t) tells you its value t years later under whatever interest rule is in force. The effective annual interest rate i is the amount that one unit invested at the start of a year has grown by over that year, expressed as a fraction of the amount present at the start of the year — it is a rate per period, not a dollar amount, and by convention refers to a full one-year period unless stated otherwise.

Accumulation FunctionA(t) = A(0)·a(t)

Nearly every problem in this topic reduces to identifying three of the four quantities — interest rate, number of periods, present value, and future (accumulated) value — and solving for the fourth. Once you can rearrange FV = PV(1+i)n (or its simple-interest counterpart) to isolate whichever variable is missing, you have the core skill this chapter is testing. Solving for FV or PV is pure substitution; solving for n requires taking a logarithm of both sides, since the unknown sits in an exponent; and solving for i requires taking an n-th root (or, for messier multi-payment problems in Chapter 4, some combination of algebra and trial-and-error). Recognizing which of these three techniques a problem calls for, just from the position of the unknown in the equation, will save you a great deal of time under exam pressure.

Simple Interest

Under simple interest, only the original principal earns interest — interest earned in prior periods is set aside and not itself reinvested to earn further interest. The accumulated value therefore grows linearly in time: each additional year adds the same fixed dollar amount of interest, equal to i times the original principal. This makes simple interest easy to reason about, but it also means simple interest understates growth over long periods compared to a world where interest is put back to work.

Simple Interest Accumulationa(t) = 1 + it

Here t = 3.5 years, so a(3.5) = 1 + (0.055)(3.5) = 1.1925. The accumulated value is

Compound Interest

Under compound interest, each period's interest is added to the balance and itself earns interest in future periods — interest earns interest. Because the base being multiplied grows every period, the accumulation function is exponential rather than linear in time. This is the standard model used for essentially every multi-year problem on the exam, and it is the model implicitly assumed whenever a problem simply says "invested at rate i" without specifying "simple interest."

Compound Interest Accumulationa(t) = (1 + i)t
Simple interest and compound interest agree exactly at t = 0 and at t = 1. For t > 1, compound interest always produces a strictly larger accumulated value (for i > 0); for 0 < t < 1, simple interest actually accumulates slightly faster. Many short-term instruments (T-bills, short bank notes) are quoted on a simple-interest basis for exactly this reason. As a quick mental estimate for how long compound growth takes to double an investment, dividing 72 by the interest rate (expressed as a whole number, e.g. 6 for 6%) gives a rough number of years — a fast sanity check you can use to see whether an exam answer is in the right ballpark before you trust your algebra.
Example 1.2 — Compound vs. Simple Growth Over a Long Horizon

Marcus invests $10,000 for 8 years. Compare the accumulated value if the fund earns (a) 4.75% effective annual compound interest, versus (b) 4.75% simple interest.

Show Solution

(a) Compound: AV = 10{,}000(1.0475)8. Computing (1.0475)8 ≈ 1.449547, so

AV = 10{,}000 × 1.449547 = $14,495.47

(b) Simple: AV = 10{,}000(1 + 0.0475 × 8) = 10{,}000 × 1.38 = $13,800.00

The compound account ends up $695.47 ahead of the simple-interest account, illustrating why compounding matters over long horizons.

Present Value and the Discount Factor

Running the accumulation function backward answers the question "what amount today grows into $1 at time n?" This one-period discount factor is denoted v, and it is simply the reciprocal of the one-period accumulation factor (1+i). Present value calculations are used constantly on this exam — anywhere a problem asks what a future obligation or payment is "worth today," you are being asked to discount it back using vn.

Discount Factor / Present Valuev = 1/(1+i)    PV = FV·vn = FV(1+i)-n
Example 1.3 — Present Value of a Future Lump Sum

A trust must pay a beneficiary $5,000 in exactly 6 years. If money is worth 6% effective annually, how much must be set aside today?

Show Solution

PV = 5000·v6 = 5000(1.06)-6. Since (1.06)6 ≈ 1.418519,

PV = 5000 / 1.418519 ≈ $3,524.80

Net Present Value

When a transaction involves several cash flows rather than a single lump sum, the net present value (NPV) at a chosen interest rate is simply the sum of the present values of every inflow minus the sum of the present values of every outflow. A positive NPV at the required rate of return means the cash-inflow side is worth more today than the cash-outflow side — a favorable deal; a negative NPV signals the opposite. The interest rate used to discount the cash flows in an NPV calculation is often called the required rate of return, hurdle rate, or cost of capital, depending on the context, but mathematically it plays exactly the role of i in the discount factor. NPV appears again as a natural extension of the equation of value in Chapter 4, where it is used to evaluate whether a stream of investment cash flows is worth undertaking.

Net Present ValueNPV = Σ CFt·vt  (inflows positive, outflows negative)

Inflation and the Real Rate of Interest

The interest rate quoted on an account (the nominal-in-the-inflation-sense rate, sometimes just called the money rate) measures growth in dollar terms. If prices are also rising at inflation rate f, part of that dollar growth merely keeps pace with rising prices rather than adding to what the money can actually buy. The real rate of interest r strips out the effect of inflation and measures growth in genuine purchasing power. Intuitively, an investor who earns 7% while prices climb 3% has not actually doubled their buying power's growth rate by earning "7% minus 3% = 4%" — that additive shortcut is only an approximation. The exact relationship compounds the two effects together, since both are multiplicative growth factors applied to the same starting amount.

Real / Nominal Interest Relationship1 + i = (1 + r)(1 + f)    r = (1+i)/(1+f) − 1
Example 1.4 — Finding the Real Rate of Return

A savings account credits 7% effective annual interest while the inflation rate over the same year runs at 3%. Find the real rate of interest earned.

Show Solution

r = 1.07/1.03 − 1 ≈ 1.038835 − 1 = 0.038835

The real rate of return is approximately 3.88% — noticeably less than the quoted 7%, because roughly 3% of that growth was absorbed by rising prices.

Watch the vocabulary carefully: the "nominal rate of interest" in the inflation/real-rate context (Chapter 1) is a completely different concept from the "nominal rate convertible m-thly" i(m) you'll meet in Chapter 2. Both are called "nominal," but one is about inflation and the other is about compounding frequency. Exam problems will tell you which is meant from context — read carefully.

2. Nominal & Effective Rates

Learning Outcomes Covered: Define nominal interest and discount rates convertible m-thly, effective discount rate, and force of interest; given any one of effective rate, nominal rate, discount rate, or force of interest, compute the others.

Nominal Interest Rate Convertible m-thly

Financial institutions rarely advertise the effective annual rate directly; instead they quote a rate "convertible" or "compounded" some number of times per year — monthly on a credit card, semiannually on a bond coupon, daily on some savings accounts. A rate i(m) convertible m-thly means interest is credited m times per year at a periodic rate of i(m)/m per period, and that periodic rate then compounds on itself just like ordinary compound interest, only over a fraction of a year at a time. The whole point of Chapter 2 is that all of these different quotations — nominal, effective, discount — describe the exact same underlying growth of money, just packaged differently, and you must be able to translate fluently between them.

Nominal ↔ Effective Conversion1 + i = (1 + i(m)/m)m    i(m) = m[(1+i)1/m − 1]
Common trap: i(m) is not the periodic rate — it must be divided by m first. Also, for a fixed i, i(m) gets smaller as m increases (more frequent compounding needs a lower quoted rate to reach the same effective annual growth).
Example 2.1 — Nominal to Effective

A credit union CD advertises 9% convertible monthly. Find the effective annual rate.

Show Solution

The monthly rate is 0.09/12 = 0.0075. Then

1 + i = (1.0075)12 ≈ 1.093807

so the effective annual rate is i ≈ 9.381% — noticeably above the quoted 9% because of monthly compounding.

Example 2.2 — Effective to Nominal

An investment earns an effective annual rate of 8%. Find the equivalent nominal annual rate convertible quarterly.

Show Solution

i(4) = 4[(1.08)1/4 − 1]. Since (1.08)0.25 ≈ 1.019427,

i(4) = 4(0.019427) ≈ 0.077706

The equivalent nominal rate convertible quarterly is i(4) ≈ 7.771%.

Effective and Nominal Discount Rates

Interest can also be quoted as a discount taken out up front rather than added at the end — this is how many short-term notes and Treasury bills are priced. The effective annual discount rate d is the amount of interest paid at the start of the year, per unit of the amount actually received (the maturity value), so a lender who advances 1 − d today collects exactly $1 one year later. Because d is charged against the larger future value while i is charged against the smaller present value, it is always true that d < i whenever both are positive — a useful sanity check when you convert between the two and want to make sure your answer is on the right side of the inequality.

Effective Discount Rated = 1 − v = iv = i/(1+i)    i = d/(1−d)
Example 2.3 — Pricing a Discount Note

A bank buys a $1,000 face-value, one-year zero-coupon note from a client at an effective annual discount rate of 6.5%. Find the price paid today and the equivalent effective annual interest rate.

Show Solution

Price: Price = 1000(1 − d) = 1000(0.935) = $935.00

Equivalent interest rate: i = d/(1-d) = 0.065/0.935 ≈ 0.069519, so i ≈ 6.95%. Check: 935 × 1.069519 ≈ 1000.00. ✓

Just as interest can be compounded m-thly, discount can be applied m-thly. A nominal discount rate d(m) convertible m-thly means a discount of d(m)/m is deducted at the start of each of the m sub-periods.

Nominal Discount Conversion1 − d = (1 − d(m)/m)m    1 + i = (1 − d(m)/m)−m
Example 2.4 — Nominal Discount Rate to Effective Interest Rate

A lender quotes a nominal discount rate of 7.2% convertible monthly. Find the equivalent effective annual interest rate.

Show Solution

The monthly discount rate is 0.072/12 = 0.006. Then 1 − d = (1 − 0.006)12 = (0.994)12 ≈ 0.930328, so

1 + i = 1/0.930328 ≈ 1.074897

The equivalent effective annual interest rate is i ≈ 7.49%.

The Rate Conversion Wheel

All five ways of describing the same underlying growth — effective rate i, nominal rate i(m), effective discount d, nominal discount d(m), and force of interest δ (Chapter 3) — are tied together through 1+i. The table below collects the formula for reaching 1+i from each quantity, which is the fastest way to pivot between any two of them: convert what you're given into 1+i, then convert 1+i into whatever you need.

Converting Any Rate Quotation to (1 + i)
GivenExpression for (1 + i)
Effective annual rate i1 + i
Nominal rate i(m) convertible m-thly(1 + i(m)/m)m
Effective discount rate d(1 − d)−1
Nominal discount d(m) convertible m-thly(1 − d(m)/m)−m
Force of interest δeδ
On the actual exam, the fastest workflow is almost always: convert whatever rate you're given into an effective annual rate i first, solve the entire problem using i, and only convert back to a different quotation at the very last step if the question demands it. Trying to work directly in i(m) or d(m) throughout invites sign and exponent errors.

It is worth noticing a pattern in the conversion table: as the compounding frequency m increases without bound, both i(m) and d(m) converge to the same limiting value — the force of interest δ. This is exactly what you would expect, since compounding infinitely often (continuous compounding) is precisely what the force of interest represents. In practice, i(m) decreases toward δ as m grows, while d(m) increases toward δ from below, with δ sandwiched between every nominal rate and every nominal discount rate quoted at any frequency.

3. Force of Interest

Learning Outcomes Covered: Define force of interest as an instantaneous rate of growth; convert between force of interest and effective/nominal/discount rates; accumulate and discount amounts under a time-varying force of interest using integration.

Force of Interest as an Instantaneous Rate

Every rate we've discussed so far — effective, nominal, discount — describes growth over a discrete chunk of time: a year, a month, a quarter. The force of interest instead describes the rate of growth of the accumulation function at a single, infinitesimally short instant, relative to the current balance at that instant. It is the continuous-time analogue of an effective rate, and it is exactly how economists and actuaries model "continuous compounding." Formally, it is the logarithmic derivative of a(t):

Force of Interest — Definitionδ(t) = a'(t)/a(t) = d/dt [ln a(t)]

Constant Force of Interest

When δ(t) = δ is constant, integrating the definition above recovers ordinary compound interest with continuous compounding, and gives a clean bridge back to the effective annual rate i.

Constant Force ↔ Effective Ratea(t) = eδt    1 + i = eδ    δ = ln(1+i)

This constant-force identity is the reason δ is often the most convenient rate to work with when a problem chains together many years of growth or requires taking roots and powers repeatedly: because δ sits inside a pure exponential with no periodic compounding to keep track of, force-of-interest arithmetic reduces to ordinary addition and multiplication of exponents rather than repeated exponentiation of (1+i). This is also precisely why δ is always strictly less than the effective rate i for any positive rate: ln(1+i) is always less than i itself.

Example 3.1 — Continuous Compounding

A fund credits a constant force of interest of 5%. Find the accumulated value of a $3,000 deposit after 10 years, and the equivalent effective annual rate.

Show Solution

AV = 3000·e(0.05)(10) = 3000·e0.5. Since e0.5 ≈ 1.648721,

AV = 3000 × 1.648721 ≈ $4,946.16

The equivalent effective annual rate is i = e0.05 − 1 ≈ 0.051271, or about 5.13%.

Variable Force of Interest

In reality, market interest rates drift up and down — a fund might earn a higher force of interest in some years and a lower one in others. When the force of interest changes with time, we can no longer just multiply by a single constant factor — we must integrate the force over the interval in question to find the total proportional growth, and then exponentiate that total to get back to an actual accumulation factor. This is the single most important formula in this chapter, and it generalizes every constant-force result above as the special case where δ(s) happens not to depend on s.

Accumulation Under Variable Forcea(t) = exp(∫0t δ(s) ds)    A(t2)/A(t1) = exp(∫t1t2 δ(s) ds)
Example 3.2 — Accumulating Under an Increasing Force of Interest

A fund's force of interest is given by δ(t) = 0.04 + 0.002t for 0 ≤ t ≤ 5. Find the accumulated value of a $1,000 deposit made at time 0, valued at time 5.

Show Solution

First integrate the force over [0,5]:

∫05 (0.04 + 0.002t) dt = 0.04(5) + 0.002(52/2) = 0.20 + 0.025 = 0.225

Then AV = 1000·e0.225 ≈ 1000 × 1.252323 = $1,252.32.

Example 3.3 — Discounting Under a Decreasing Force of Interest

Suppose instead δ(t) = 0.08 − 0.01t for 0 ≤ t ≤ 3. Find the present value of $5,000 due at time 3.

Show Solution

∫03 (0.08 − 0.01t) dt = 0.08(3) − 0.01(32/2) = 0.24 − 0.045 = 0.195

So a(3) = e0.195 ≈ 1.215308, and

PV = 5000 × e−0.195 ≈ 5000 × 0.822835 = $4,114.17

The most common error with variable force of interest is exponentiating too early — students sometimes compute eδ(t) at a single instant and try to multiply, rather than integrating δ(s) over the whole interval first and exponentiating only the total. Always integrate first, exponentiate last, and double-check your antiderivative before plugging in the limits.

It's also worth remembering that the force of interest is defined for every instant, so it applies equally well to a fund whose value fluctuates due to both interest credited and deposits or withdrawals made by the account holder — as long as you're only asked about the interest-driven growth rate itself, δ(t) depends only on how fast the fund is compounding at that moment, not on how large the balance happens to be. This is what makes it possible to quote a single force-of-interest function for an entire market or index, independent of how much any individual investor has on deposit.

4. Equations of Value

Learning Outcomes Covered: Set up an equation of value for a collection of cash flows occurring at different times, at a specified interest rate; solve such an equation for an unknown quantity such as a payment amount or an interest rate.

Comparing Cash Flows at Different Times

Because money at different points in time is not directly comparable, any transaction involving multiple payments must be evaluated by moving every payment to a single common date — the valuation date (or comparison date) — using the accumulation and discount functions developed in Chapters 1 through 3. The resulting equality between the value of what is received and the value of what is paid is called the equation of value. Everything you have learned so far in this part — simple and compound accumulation, present value, nominal and effective conversions, and force of interest — exists to support this single unifying tool: once cash flows are correctly moved to a common date, the rest is algebra.

Equation of ValueΣ (value of inflows at date C) = Σ (value of outflows at date C)

Choosing a Comparison Date

A remarkable — and very useful — fact is that the interest rate (or unknown payment) you solve for from an equation of value does not depend on which date C you pick, provided every cash flow is moved consistently using the same rate. Multiplying an entire equation by a constant power of (1+i) doesn't change its solution, since both sides scale by the same factor. In practice, you should pick whichever date makes the algebra simplest — often time 0 (so every term is a present value, and you don't need to reason about negative exponents at all) or the date of the last cash flow (so no term ever needs to be discounted, only accumulated). This freedom to choose is a convenience, not a new piece of theory — it follows immediately from the fact that a(t) is a genuine exponential-type function, so moving a value forward and then back by the same amount of time always returns you to where you started.

Example 4.1 — Solving for an Unknown Payment

Deon borrows $10,000 today at an effective annual rate of 5%. He will repay the loan with a payment of $4,000 at the end of year 2 and a final payment X at the end of year 5. Find X.

Show Solution

Valuing everything at time 0, with v = 1/1.05:

10{,}000 = 4000v2 + Xv5

Now v2 ≈ 0.907029 and v5 ≈ 0.783526, so 4000v2 ≈ 3628.12. Then

Xv5 = 10{,}000 − 3628.12 = 6371.88   ⟹   X = 6371.88 / 0.783526 ≈ $8,132.32

Example 4.2 — Solving for an Unknown Rate

Angela invests $2,000 today in exchange for a single payment of $2,700 four years from now. Find the effective annual rate of return implied by this transaction.

Show Solution

The equation of value at time 0 is 2000(1+i)4 = 2700, so (1+i)4 = 1.35 and

1 + i = 1.351/4 ≈ 1.077913

The implied rate of return is i ≈ 7.79%.

Example 4.3 — Replacing Two Debts With One (Comparison Date Doesn't Matter)

A company owes $1,000 due in 1 year and $2,000 due in 3 years. It wishes to replace both obligations with a single payment X due in exactly 2 years. Money is worth 6% effective annually. Find X, and confirm the answer is the same whether you value the equation at time 2 or at time 0.

Show Solution

Valuing at t = 2 (accumulate the year-1 debt one year forward, discount the year-3 debt one year back):

X = 1000(1.06) + 2000(1.06)−1 = 1060.00 + 1886.79 = $2,946.79

Valuing at t = 0 instead: 1000v + 2000v3 = Xv2. Computing the left side, 1000v ≈ 943.40 and 2000v3 ≈ 1679.24, for a total of 2622.64. Then X = 2622.64 × (1.06)2 = 2622.64 × 1.1236 ≈ $2,946.79 — the same answer, exactly as the time-invariance property predicts.

Example 4.4 — Net Present Value of a Project

A small business is considering a project requiring an initial outlay of $8,000 today, which is expected to return $3,000 at the end of year 1, $3,000 at the end of year 2, and $4,000 at the end of year 3. The company's required rate of return is 7% effective annually. Compute the NPV and state whether the project should be accepted.

Show Solution

NPV = −8000 + 3000v + 3000v2 + 4000v3, with v = 1/1.07.

Using v ≈ 0.934579, v2 ≈ 0.873439, v3 ≈ 0.816298:

PV of inflows ≈ 2803.74 + 2620.32 + 3265.19 = $8,689.25

NPV = 8689.25 − 8000 = $689.25

Since NPV is positive at the required 7% rate, the project should be accepted.

Before writing a single equation, sketch a simple timeline showing every cash flow with its sign (inflow or outflow) and the time it occurs. Almost every equation-of-value error on this topic — a sign flipped, a payment moved to the wrong side, an exponent off by one period — traces back to skipping this step.
Do not mix interest rates or force of interest definitions within a single equation of value. If a problem quotes a nominal rate convertible monthly for one cash flow and an effective annual rate context for another, convert everything to the same effective annual rate first, then build the equation.

Finally, remember that an equation of value is only as good as the sign convention behind it. It helps to fix a rule at the start of a problem — for instance, "money received by the party we're tracking is positive, money paid out is negative" — and stick to it for every term in the equation. When a problem involves a loan, the amount borrowed is an inflow to the borrower at the start and every repayment is an outflow later; from the lender's perspective every sign simply flips. Keeping straight whose perspective the equation of value is written from is just as important as getting the discount factors right.

Part II — Annuities / Non-Contingent Cash Flows (20–30%)

Part I dealt with a single lump sum moving through time. Real financial transactions almost never look like that — mortgages, car loans, retirement savings plans, bond coupons, and lease agreements all involve a stream of payments spread across many dates. This part builds the toolkit for valuing those streams. We start with the plain-vanilla case (a fixed payment, once a year, for a fixed number of years) and then generalize in three directions: payments that never stop (perpetuities), payments that arrive more often than once a year (m-thly and continuous annuities), and payments whose size changes from one period to the next in a predictable pattern (arithmetic and geometric growth). By the end of Part II you should be able to look at almost any "non-contingent" cash-flow schedule — meaning nothing about whether or when a payment is made depends on some random future event like a death or a default — and reduce it to a present value, a future value, or a value at any point in between. Because this is one of the two heaviest-weighted topics on the actual exam, this part is deliberately the longest and most example-dense section of the manual. Work every example by hand before reading the solution; annuity algebra becomes second nature only through repetition. As you move through the six chapters, notice that they build on each other in a deliberate sequence: Chapter 1 establishes the two timing conventions that every later formula inherits; Chapter 2 pushes the term to infinity; Chapter 3 slices the payment interval finer instead; Chapters 4 and 5 let the payment size itself move over time, first linearly and then by a constant percentage; and Chapter 6 shows how to combine all of the above to handle whatever irregular schedule an exam question throws at you. Mastering this part is less about memorizing fourteen separate formulas and more about internalizing this one progression, since nearly every closed form here can be re-derived on the spot from the geometric-series logic introduced in Chapter 1.

1. Annuity-Immediate & Annuity-Due

Learning Outcomes Covered: Define annuity-immediate and annuity-due and distinguish their payment timing; define the term of an annuity; given any four of {present value, future value/current value, interest rate, payment amount, number of payments}, solve for the fifth for a level-payment annuity in either timing convention.

A quick notational note before we start: the actuarial symbol for "the present value of an n-payment annuity" is normally written as a lowercase a with a small right angle drawn over the n. Plain HTML has no clean way to draw that angle mark, so throughout this manual we write it as an⌐ — a subscript n followed by the corner symbol ⌐ standing in for the angle. Whenever you see an⌐, sn⌐, än⌐, or similar, read it exactly as you would the standard angled symbol from your calculator's TVM keys or from any other FM reference.

1.1 Two Timing Conventions

An annuity-immediate is a series of equal payments made at the end of each period, for a fixed number of periods called the term of the annuity. If someone tells you "$500 per month for three years" without further qualification, assume the payments land at the end of each month — this is the default convention on the exam unless the problem explicitly says otherwise. An annuity-due is the same idea but with payments at the start of each period instead. A one-year apartment lease paid at the beginning of every month is a textbook annuity-due; a bond coupon paid at the end of every six-month period is a textbook annuity-immediate.

Both are level annuities in this section, meaning every payment is the same size. Later chapters relax that assumption.

The term of an annuity is simply the number of payment periods it covers — a "10-year annuity-immediate" has a term of 10 years, meaning 10 payments. Term matters for two reasons beyond just counting payments: it fixes the exponent in every formula below, and it fixes how far the last payment sits from the valuation date. A common source of off-by-one errors is confusing the term with the number of years elapsed; for an annuity-immediate with a 10-year term, the first payment lands at the end of year 1 and the last at the end of year 10, so the entire stream spans exactly 10 years from the valuation date to the final payment — no more, no less.

One more practical note: on a financial calculator, the choice between annuity-immediate and annuity-due is usually controlled by an "END" vs. "BEGIN" (or "BGN") mode setting. Getting this setting wrong is a frequent source of exam-prep errors when practicing with a calculator, since the calculator will happily compute a due-annuity value while you believe you're looking at an immediate-annuity value. Get in the habit of checking (or resetting) the mode before every calculation.

1.2 Valuing the Annuity-Immediate

Let i be the effective interest rate per period and v = 1/(1+i) the corresponding discount factor. For n payments of 1 each, made at the end of periods 1 through n, the present value one period before the first payment (i.e., at time 0) is the sum of a geometric series of discount factors:

PV of Annuity-Immediatean⌐ = v + v2 + ... + vn = (1 − vn)/i

The future value, measured at time n (the moment of the last payment), is found either by accumulating each payment forward or by multiplying the PV by (1+i)n:

AV of Annuity-Immediatesn⌐ = [(1+i)n − 1]/i

Both formulas come straight from the finite geometric series formula — there is nothing to memorize beyond that series identity and the definitions of v and i. If you ever forget which one is which, rebuild it: write out the cash flow timeline, discount or accumulate every payment to the valuation date, and sum.

1.3 Valuing the Annuity-Due

Shifting every payment one period earlier multiplies every discounted or accumulated term by exactly (1+i), since each payment now has one fewer period to be discounted (or one extra period to accumulate). That gives the clean conversion rule that connects due and immediate annuities of the same term:

PV of Annuity-Dueän⌐ = an⌐(1+i) = (1 − vn)/d
AV of Annuity-Dues̈n⌐ = sn⌐(1+i)

where d = i/(1+i) = 1 − v is the effective discount rate from Part I. There is a second useful identity worth memorizing alongside the first: an n-payment due annuity is the same as "one payment today, plus an (n−1)-payment immediate annuity starting next period," so

Due–Immediate Shift Identityän⌐ = 1 + a(n−1)⌐ and s̈n⌐ = s(n+1)⌐ − 1

Either identity — the (1+i) multiplier or the shift-by-one-payment logic — will get you from immediate to due or back. Use whichever is faster for the problem in front of you.

Common Trap — Immediate vs. Due Confusion. The single most common arithmetic slip on annuity problems is applying the wrong timing convention: using an⌐ when the payments actually start immediately (time 0), or vice versa. Before touching a formula, sketch the timeline and mark exactly where the first payment falls relative to the valuation date. If the first payment is one full period after the valuation date, it's immediate; if it coincides with the valuation date, it's due. This thirty-second habit prevents the majority of sign and magnitude errors on this topic.

1.4 Current Value at an Intermediate Date

The syllabus also expects you to value an annuity at a date that is neither the very start nor the very end of the payment stream — its current value. The technique is simply an extension of the equation-of-value idea from Part I: accumulate every payment already made up to the valuation date, discount every payment still to come back to that same date, and add the two pieces together. If a valuation date falls immediately after the t-th payment of an n-payment annuity-immediate of 1 per period, its current value is

Current Value (mid-stream)CVt = st⌐ + a(n−t)⌐

You can always double-check a current-value answer by computing the value at time 0 first and then accumulating the whole thing forward by (1+i)t — both routes must agree, since they describe the same cash flows valued at the same instant.

Example 1.1 — Accumulating Retirement Contributions

Maya contributes $4,000 to a retirement account at the end of every year for 20 years. The account earns 6% effective annual interest. Find the accumulated value of the account immediately after the 20th deposit.

Show Solution

This is a straightforward annuity-immediate future value. With i = 0.06 and n = 20: (1.06)20 = 3.207135, so s20⌐ = (3.207135 − 1)/0.06 = 36.7856. The accumulated value is 4000 × 36.7856 = $147,142.36.

Example 1.2 — Solving for the Payment on a Loan

A credit union lends $50,000 to a small-business owner, to be repaid by equal annual payments at the end of each year for 15 years at an annual effective rate of 7%. Find the required payment.

Show Solution

The loan amount is the present value of the payment annuity: 50,000 = P × a15⌐ at 7%. With v15 = 1.07−15 = 0.362446, a15⌐ = (1 − 0.362446)/0.07 = 9.107914. Then P = 50,000/9.107914 = $5,489.65.

Example 1.3 — Solving for the Number of Payments

Devon deposits $2,500 at the end of each year into a fund earning 5% effective annually, hoping to accumulate at least $50,000. After how many deposits will the fund first reach or exceed $50,000?

Show Solution

We need the smallest integer n with 2500 × sn⌐ ≥ 50,000, i.e. sn⌐ ≥ 20. Solving [(1.05)n − 1]/0.05 = 20 continuously gives (1.05)n = 2, so n = ln(2)/ln(1.05) ≈ 14.21. Since deposits only happen at whole years, check the integers on either side: s14⌐ = 19.5986 gives an accumulated value of 2500 × 19.5986 = $48,996.58 — not enough. s15⌐ = 21.5786 gives 2500 × 21.5786 = $53,946.41, which clears the target. So Devon needs 15 deposits.

Example 1.4 — Annuity-Due Present Value

A donor wants to fund a scholarship that pays $10,000 at the beginning of each year for 4 years. If the fund can earn 4% annual effective interest, how much must the donor deposit today?

Show Solution

Beginning-of-year payments make this an annuity-due: we need 10,000 × ä4⌐ at 4%. First find a4⌐: v4 = 1.04−4 = 0.854804, so a4⌐ = (1 − 0.854804)/0.04 = 3.629895. Then ä4⌐ = 3.629895 × 1.04 = 3.775091. The required deposit is 10,000 × 3.775091 = $37,750.91.

Example 1.5 — Solving for the Interest Rate

A borrower takes out a $20,000 loan and agrees to repay it with 10 equal annual payments of $2,800, starting one year from now. Estimate the annual effective interest rate the lender is charging.

Show Solution

We need i such that a10⌐ = 20,000/2,800 = 7.142857. This equation has no clean algebraic solution for i, so we bracket it. At i = 6%, a10⌐ = 7.360087; at i = 7%, a10⌐ = 7.023582. Our target sits between these, closer to the 7% side. Linear interpolation: fraction = (7.360087 − 7.142857)/(7.360087 − 7.023582) = 0.6456, so i ≈ 6% + 0.6456% = 6.65%. (A financial calculator's TVM solver would confirm this to more decimal places, but interpolation between two bracketing rates is exactly the technique the exam expects when a closed-form solution for i isn't available.)

Example 1.6 — Current Value Mid-Stream

A lender holds a 10-year annuity-immediate of $2,000 per year at 6% effective annual interest (this is the payment stream backing a loan the lender originated). Immediately after receiving the 4th payment, the lender wants to sell the remaining claim plus knows the value of what's already been collected — in short, find the value of the entire original cash-flow schedule, past and future, as of the date right after payment 4.

Show Solution

Current value = accumulated value of the first 4 payments + present value (as of time 4) of the remaining 6 payments: CV4 = 2000 × s4⌐ + 2000 × a6⌐. At 6%: s4⌐ = (1.064 − 1)/0.06 = 4.374617 and a6⌐ = (1 − 1.06−6)/0.06 = 4.917317. So CV4 = 2000(4.374617) + 2000(4.917317) = 8,749.23 + 9,834.63 = $18,583.87. Check: the time-0 value of the full stream is 2000 × a10⌐ = 2000(7.360087) = 14,720.17; accumulating that forward 4 years gives 14,720.17 × 1.064 = 14,720.17 × 1.262477 = $18,583.87 — the two approaches agree, as they must.

Memory Aid. Due annuities are always worth more than the corresponding immediate annuities of the same term and payment, because every payment is received earlier. So än⌐ > an⌐ and s̈n⌐ > sn⌐ always — if your algebra produces the opposite, you've made an error somewhere.

2. Perpetuities

Learning Outcomes Covered: Define a level perpetuity-immediate and perpetuity-due; compute the present value of a perpetuity, including deferred perpetuities; use the perpetuity formula as a quick sanity check on long-term annuity values.

2.1 Payments That Never Stop

A perpetuity is an annuity with no final payment — it continues forever. That may sound like an unusual real-world object, but perpetuity-style cash flows show up constantly in practice: certain endowed funds are designed to pay out a fixed amount every year in perpetuity while the principal remains untouched, and preferred stock with no maturity date is often modeled as a perpetuity of dividends.

To find the present value, take the annuity-immediate formula an⌐ = (1 − vn)/i and let n → ∞. Since 0 < v < 1 whenever i > 0, the term vn shrinks to 0, leaving a strikingly simple result:

PV of Perpetuity-Immediatea∞⌐ = 1/i

Applying the same due-annuity multiplier from Chapter 1 (multiply by (1+i), or equivalently divide by d instead of i) gives the perpetuity-due value:

PV of Perpetuity-Dueä∞⌐ = 1/d

There is no future-value formula for a perpetuity — a stream with no end never has a final accumulation date, so "future value of a perpetuity" is not a meaningful question.

Perpetuities also show up as a modeling device even when the real-world cash flow eventually stops. If a project or a bond's payments are expected to continue for, say, 60 or 100 years, analysts frequently value it as a perpetuity anyway, because — as Example 2.4 below demonstrates — the difference between a very-long-term annuity and a true perpetuity is often smaller than the uncertainty in the other assumptions feeding the model. Recognizing this lets you use 1/i or 1/d as a fast mental estimate before reaching for a calculator on any annuity with a long term.

Common Trap — Mixing Up i and d. It is tempting to reuse 1/i for both timing conventions. Resist that: the due perpetuity is valued at the same instant as its first payment, which makes it worth exactly one extra "current" payment relative to the immediate case, and pushing that logic through the algebra is precisely what converts 1/i into 1/d. A fast consistency check: since d < i for any positive rate, 1/d must be larger than 1/i — matching the fact that due annuities are always worth more.

It is worth pausing on why a payment stream that literally never ends still has a finite present value. Each successive payment is discounted by one more factor of v, and because v < 1, those discount factors shrink toward zero geometrically. The payments arriving centuries from now are worth virtually nothing today, so the infinite sum still converges to a finite number — the same reasoning that makes the infinite geometric series formula 1 + v + v2 + ... = 1/(1-v) valid in the first place. This only works because i > 0; a perpetuity formula is meaningless at a zero or negative interest rate, since the terms would no longer shrink.

2.2 Deferred Perpetuities

If the first payment doesn't start right away, value the perpetuity as of the date of its first payment using the ordinary formula, then discount that single lump sum back to today using the Part I compound-interest tools.

Example 2.1 — Basic Perpetuity-Immediate

An endowed prize fund pays out $6,000 at the end of every year, forever, and the fund earns a stable 5% effective annual return. How much principal must the fund hold today to sustain this indefinitely?

Show Solution

PV = 6,000/0.05 = $120,000. As a check: at 5% interest, $120,000 earns exactly $6,000 in interest every year, which is paid out, leaving the principal untouched forever — exactly the defining property of a perpetuity.

Example 2.2 — Perpetuity-Due

A retiree wants to receive $3,000 at the start of every year for the rest of her life, structured (for planning purposes) as a perpetuity, with the fund earning 6% effective annually. What lump sum is required today?

Show Solution

d = i/(1+i) = 0.06/1.06 = 0.0566038, so ä∞⌐ = 1/d = 1.06/0.06 = 17.66667. The required lump sum is 3,000 × 17.66667 = $53,000.00.

Example 2.3 — Deferred Perpetuity-Due

A university plans to fund a scholarship that pays $5,000 at the start of each year forever, but for administrative reasons the very first payment won't be made until 7 years from today (and then continues every year after that). At 6% effective annual interest, what should the university deposit today?

Show Solution

Because this is a perpetuity-due, its value formula 1/d is measured as of the same date as its first payment — here, time 7. So the value at time 7 is 5,000/d = 5,000/0.0566038 = 88,333.33. Discount that lump sum back 7 years at 6%: v7 = 1.06−7 = 0.665057, giving a value today of 88,333.33 × 0.665057 = $58,746.71.

Example 2.4 — Valuing a Consol-Style Bond

A century-old "consol" bond with a $1,000 face value pays an annual coupon of 5% of face value at the end of every year, forever (consols are historically real instruments with no maturity date). If investors currently demand a 6% effective annual return on bonds of similar risk, find the fair market price of the consol.

Show Solution

The annual coupon is 5% × 1,000 = $50, paid forever, valued at the market rate of 6% (not the coupon rate). Price = 50/0.06 = $833.33. Notice this is below the $1,000 face value, since the bond's fixed 5% coupon is less attractive than the 6% investors could otherwise earn — exactly the same logic used to price ordinary bonds trading at a discount, applied to an infinite-term instrument.

Example 2.5 — Perpetuities as a Sanity Check

A pension actuary is valuing a 50-year annuity-immediate of $1 per year at 10% effective interest and wants a quick mental check that the calculator answer is in the right neighborhood before moving on. Compare a50⌐ to the perpetuity value 1/i.

Show Solution

a50⌐ = (1 − 1.10−50)/0.10. Since 1.1050 ≈ 117.39, v50 ≈ 0.008519, giving a50⌐ ≈ 9.9148. The perpetuity value is 1/0.10 = 10. The two differ by less than 1%. This illustrates a genuinely useful exam technique: once n is large relative to 1/i, a finite annuity's value is extremely close to the corresponding perpetuity's value, since the tail payments contribute almost nothing in present-value terms. If your computed annuity value is wildly different from 1/i for a long-term annuity, suspect an arithmetic error.

3. Annuities Payable m-thly & Continuously

Learning Outcomes Covered: Define annuities with payments made m times per year and annuities with continuous payment streams; compute present and future values using nominal rates convertible m-thly and the force of interest; convert correctly between annual, m-thly, and continuous payment frequencies.

3.1 Splitting the Year into m Pieces

So far every annuity has paid once per year. In practice, payments often arrive monthly, quarterly, or semiannually. If a total of 1 per year is instead paid in m equal installments of 1/m at the end of every 1/m-th of a year, for n years, the natural rate to use is the nominal annual rate convertible m-thly, i(m), which by definition satisfies (1 + i(m)/m)m = 1+i. Substituting the per-period rate i(m)/m and the mn payments into the ordinary annuity-immediate formula and simplifying yields:

PV, Annuity-Immediate Payable m-thlyan⌐(m) = (1 − vn)/i(m)

Note that an⌐(m) denotes the present value of a total of 1 per year paid m-thly — not 1 per installment. If the actual payment is 1/m made m times a year, its total present value is exactly an⌐(m); if the payment amount is some other annual total X, the PV is X × an⌐(m). Exactly the same pattern extends to the due version, using the nominal discount rate d(m) in place of i(m):

PV, Annuity-Due Payable m-thlyän⌐(m) = (1 − vn)/d(m)

and future values follow by multiplying by (1+i)n exactly as in Chapter 1.

3.2 Pushing m to Infinity: Continuous Annuities

As m grows without bound, payments blend into a smooth, unbroken stream — money flowing in continuously rather than in discrete installments. In this limit, i(m) converges to the force of interest δ = ln(1+i) introduced in Part I, giving the cleanest formula in this chapter:

PV of Continuous Annuityān⌐ = (1 − vn)/δ

A continuous perpetuity, by the same limiting argument used in Chapter 2, is simply ā∞⌐ = 1/δ.

3.3 The Full Spectrum

Annual, m-thly, and continuous annuities are really just three points on a single spectrum of payment frequency, all describing the same total annual cash flow spread differently across the year. For a fixed effective annual rate i, increasing the payment frequency m increases the present value of an immediate-style annuity slightly (payments arrive sooner, on average) and always satisfies the ordering i < i(2) < i(4) < ... < δ < ... < d(4) < d(2) < d — the nominal rates convertible more frequently sit closer to δ from either side. This ordering is a handy way to sanity-check a computed i(m) or d(m) value.

The table below makes the spectrum concrete. It shows the present value of a 5-year, $1-per-year annuity-immediate at an effective annual rate of 6%, valued four different ways depending on how the $1 is split across the year:

Payment FrequencyRelevant RatePresent Value Factor
Annually (m = 1)i = 6.0000%a5⌐ = 4.212364
Quarterly (m = 4)i(4) = 5.8695%a5⌐(4) = 4.305991

The present value climbs steadily as payments are split more finely, but by a shrinking amount each time — the jump from annual to quarterly is far larger than the jump from monthly to continuous. This diminishing-returns pattern is typical, and it's why the exam rarely expects you to distinguish between, say, monthly and continuous payments to more than a few cents of precision.

Common Trap — Treating i(m) as an Effective Rate. The nominal rate i(m) is not the effective rate over an m-thly period — it is m times that effective rate, by definition. The actual per-period effective rate is i(m)/m. Plugging i(m) directly into a formula that expects a per-period effective rate (rather than dividing by m first) is one of the most frequent mechanical errors on m-thly problems, and it is easy to miss because the resulting number often still looks "reasonable" at first glance.
Conversion Discipline. The single biggest source of errors in this chapter is plugging a monthly payment amount into a formula alongside an uncorrected nominal rate, or vice versa. Before writing any formula, settle three things: (1) how many payments per year, (2) what nominal or effective rate matches that frequency, and (3) whether you want the annuity-immediate or annuity-due version. It is almost always safest to convert everything to the per-payment-period effective rate — e.g. divide a nominal monthly-convertible rate by 12 — and then treat the problem as an ordinary single-frequency annuity in that period, only converting back to i(m) notation if the question specifically asks for it.

A car lease requires payments of $450 at the end of every month for 3 years. The dealer's financing carries a nominal annual rate of 9% convertible monthly. Find the present value of the lease payments.

A family trust pays out income continuously at a rate of $10,000 per year for 8 years. The trust's investments earn a force of interest of 6%. Find the present value of the payout stream.

Example 3.4 — Continuous Perpetuity

A foundation wants to fund a continuous stream of grant money at a rate of $50,000 per year, forever, with the endowment earning a force of interest of 5%. What is the required endowment today?

Show Solution

4. Arithmetic Increasing/Decreasing Annuities

Learning Outcomes Covered: Define arithmetically increasing and decreasing annuities, both finite-term and perpetual; derive and apply the standard closed-form formulas for their present and future values; solve for an unknown payment, increment, rate, or term in an arithmetic annuity problem.

4.1 Payments That Change by a Fixed Amount Each Period

An arithmetically increasing annuity-immediate, in its simplest standard form, pays 1 at the end of year 1, 2 at the end of year 2, 3 at the end of year 3, and so on up to a payment of n at the end of year n. Its present value is denoted (Ia)n⌐. The mirror-image decreasing annuity, denoted (Da)n⌐, pays n at the end of year 1, n−1 at the end of year 2, ..., down to a final payment of 1 at the end of year n.

There are several equivalent ways to derive the closed-form value of (Ia)n⌐; the cleanest views the increasing annuity as a stack of n overlapping level annuities. Picture n separate level annuities-immediate, each paying 1 per year: the first runs for all n years, the second for years 2 through n, the third for years 3 through n, and so on down to the last, which pays only in year n. Stack them on top of each other — in year t, exactly t of these annuities are still paying (years 1 through t of overlap), so the combined payment in year t is exactly t, which is exactly the increasing-annuity payment pattern. Summing the present values of the n stacked level annuities, an⌐ + a(n−1)⌐ + ... + a1⌐, and simplifying with the geometric series formula produces the standard closed form:

PV, Arithmetic Increasing Annuity-Immediate(Ia)n⌐ = (än⌐ − n vn)/i

The decreasing annuity can be built by noticing that a decreasing annuity plus an increasing annuity of the same term, added payment-by-payment, gives a level annuity of n+1 every year (since t + (n+1−t) = n+1 for every t). That is, (Ia)n⌐ + (Da)n⌐ = (n+1) an⌐, which rearranges directly to the decreasing-annuity formula:

PV, Arithmetic Decreasing Annuity-Immediate(Da)n⌐ = (n − an⌐)/i

Future values follow the same pattern as before — multiply the corresponding PV by (1+i)n, or work directly from the accumulated-value analogue (Is)n⌐ = (s̈n⌐ − n)/i. And exactly as in Chapter 1, shifting every payment one period earlier to convert an increasing annuity-immediate into an increasing annuity-due just multiplies through by (1+i): (Iä)n⌐ = (1+i)(Ia)n⌐, and likewise (Dä)n⌐ = (1+i)(Da)n⌐. The exam far more often tests the immediate versions, but recognizing that the due/immediate multiplier from Chapter 1 applies unchanged here will save you from re-deriving anything from scratch if a due-timing increasing annuity appears.

4.2 The Increasing Perpetuity

Letting n → ∞ in the increasing-annuity formula requires a little more care than the level-perpetuity limit, because both the payment size and the number of payments are growing. Carrying out the limit carefully (or summing the infinite series Σ t vt directly) gives:

PV of Increasing Perpetuity-Immediate(Ia)∞⌐ = 1/i + 1/i2 = 1/(i·d)

All three expressions above are algebraically identical — verify it yourself using d = i/(1+i). A decreasing perpetuity has no meaning (the payments would need to hit zero and then go negative), so there is no decreasing-perpetuity formula.

4.3 General Arithmetic Progressions

Real problems rarely use the "clean" 1, 2, 3, ..., n pattern. More often the first payment is some amount P and each subsequent payment changes by a fixed amount Q (positive for increasing, negative for decreasing), so the payment in year t is P + (t−1)Q. Writing this as (P − Q) + tQ and splitting the sum into a level piece and a scaled increasing-annuity piece gives the general formula every arithmetic-annuity problem ultimately reduces to:

PV, General Arithmetic Annuity-ImmediatePV = (P − Q) an⌐ + Q (Ia)n⌐

This single formula handles increasing annuities (Q > 0), decreasing annuities (Q < 0), and the standard "clean" cases as special instances (set P = Q = 1 to recover (Ia)n⌐ exactly, or P = n, Q = −1 to recover (Da)n⌐).

Which Formula Starts Big? A quick way to keep (Ia) and (Da) straight: "I" is for increasing, so (Ia)n⌐ starts small (payment of 1) and grows to n; "D" is for decreasing, so (Da)n⌐ starts big (payment of n) and shrinks to 1. If a problem's largest payment comes first, you almost certainly want a (Da)-based or general formula with negative Q.
Example 4.1 — Increasing Bonus Schedule

An employer promises a departing employee a series of retention bonuses: $1,000 at the end of year 1, $2,000 at the end of year 2, increasing by $1,000 each year through a final $6,000 payment at the end of year 6. At 7% effective annual interest, find the present value of this payment schedule.

Show Solution

This is exactly 1,000 × (Ia)6⌐ at 7%. First, v6 = 1.07−6 = 0.666342, a6⌐ = (1−0.666342)/0.07 = 4.766540, and ä6⌐ = 4.766540 × 1.07 = 5.100198. Then (Ia)6⌐ = (5.100198 − 6 × 0.666342)/0.07 = (5.100198 − 3.998052)/0.07 = 15.744916. Present value: 1,000 × 15.744916 = $15,744.92.

Example 4.2 — Decreasing Loan-Style Payments

A landowner sells a small parcel and agrees to receive decreasing annual payments: $10,000 at the end of year 1, $9,000 at the end of year 2, decreasing by $1,000 each year, down to a final $1,000 payment at the end of year 10. At 5% effective annual interest, find the present value.

Show Solution

This is 1,000 × (Da)10⌐ at 5%. From Chapter 1's numbers, a10⌐ = 7.721735 at 5%. So (Da)10⌐ = (10 − 7.721735)/0.05 = 45.565301. Present value: 1,000 × 45.565301 = $45,565.30.

Example 4.3 — Increasing Perpetuity

A scholarship endowment is designed to pay $1,000 at the end of year 1, $2,000 at the end of year 2, and so on, increasing by $1,000 every year forever. At 8% effective annual interest, find the present value of this commitment.

Show Solution

(Ia)∞⌐ = 1/i + 1/i2 = 1/0.08 + 1/0.0064 = 12.5 + 156.25 = 168.75. Present value: 1,000 × 168.75 = $168,750. Example 4.4 — General Arithmetic Annuity (Increasing Rent)

A commercial tenant's lease calls for rent of $12,000 at the end of year 1, increasing by $1,000 every year, for 8 years (so the year-8 payment is $19,000). At 4% effective annual interest, find the present value of the total rent obligation.

Show Solution

Here P = 12,000, Q = 1,000, n = 8. We need a8⌐ and (Ia)8⌐ at 4%. v8 = 1.04−8 = 0.730690, a8⌐ = (1−0.730690)/0.04 = 6.732745, ä8⌐ = 6.732745 × 1.04 = 7.002055, and (Ia)8⌐ = (7.002055 − 8×0.730690)/0.04 = 28.913326. Applying the general formula: PV = (12,000−1,000)(6.732745) + 1,000(28.913326) = 74,060.19 + 28,913.33 = $102,973.52.

Example 4.5 — Solving for the Unknown Increment

A pension pays $5,000 at the end of year 1, increasing by a fixed amount D every year for 15 years. At 6% effective annual interest, the present value of this pension is known to be $110,000. Find D.

Show Solution

Using the general formula with P=5,000, Q=D, n=15: 110,000 = (5,000 − D)a15⌐ + D(Ia)15⌐ = 5,000 a15⌐ + D[(Ia)15⌐ − a15⌐]. At 6%: a15⌐ = 9.712249, ä15⌐ = 10.294984, v15 = 0.417265, so (Ia)15⌐ = (10.294984 − 15×0.417265)/0.06 = 67.266800. Then (Ia)15⌐ − a15⌐ = 57.554551. Substituting: 110,000 = 5,000(9.712249) + D(57.554551) = 48,561.25 + 57.554551D. Solving: D = (110,000 − 48,561.25)/57.554551 = 61,438.75/57.554551 ≈ $1,067.49 per year.

5. Geometric Increasing/Decreasing Annuities

Learning Outcomes Covered: Define annuities whose payments grow or shrink by a constant percentage each period; value such annuities for both finite terms and perpetuities by converting to an equivalent level annuity at an adjusted rate; identify and correctly handle the special boundary case where the growth rate equals the interest rate.

5.1 Payments That Grow (or Shrink) by a Fixed Percentage

Instead of changing by a fixed dollar amount each period, a geometrically increasing (or decreasing) annuity has payments that change by a fixed percentage each period — a very natural model for cash flows meant to track inflation, salary growth, or a business's expected revenue decline. Suppose the first payment, at the end of year 1, is 1, and every subsequent payment is (1+k) times the previous one, where k is the constant growth rate per period (negative k models a shrinking stream). The payment in year t is therefore (1+k)t−1.

The key insight for valuing this stream is that a payment growing at rate k and then discounted at rate i behaves exactly like a level payment discounted at some smaller effective rate. Concretely:

Present Value, term tPVt = (1+k)t−1 vt = v × [(1+k)/(1+i)]t−1

Summing this geometric series for t = 1 to n, with common ratio r = (1+k)/(1+i), and simplifying (the algebra uses 1 − r = (i−k)/(1+i) and v(1+i)=1) collapses into a remarkably clean closed form:

PV, Geometric Annuity-Immediate (finite, k ≠ i)PV = [1 − ((1+k)/(1+i))n]/(i − k)

This formula gives the present value of a stream whose first payment is exactly 1; scale by the actual first-payment amount for any other case. Notice the strong resemblance to the ordinary level-annuity formula (1−vn)/i — a geometric annuity is really just a level annuity valued at an "effective" net discount rate of i − k in place of i in the denominator, and using the ratio (1+k)/(1+i) in place of v in the exponent.

5.2 The Geometric Perpetuity

If the growth rate is strictly less than the interest rate (k < i), then r = (1+k)/(1+i) < 1 and rn → 0 as n → ∞, so the perpetuity value converges to a very clean limit:

PV, Geometric Perpetuity-Immediate (k < i)PV = 1/(i − k)

If instead k ≥ i, the individual discounted payments never shrink toward zero (or actually grow), so the infinite sum diverges — a geometric perpetuity only has a finite value when its growth rate is strictly below the discount rate. This makes economic sense: if a cash flow's growth rate matches or beats the rate you use to discount it, an infinite stream of it is literally worth an infinite amount today.

This growing-perpetuity formula is one of the most widely used results in all of applied finance — it is the mathematical backbone of dividend-growth valuation models used to price stocks, where a company's expected dividend is projected to grow at a steady long-run rate below the required rate of return. Recognizing the formula in that guise (or in the guise of a growing lease, a growing royalty stream, or a growing pension) is a common way the exam disguises what is otherwise a direct application of the boxed formula above.

As with the arithmetic case, converting a geometric annuity-immediate to a geometric annuity-due (payments starting at time 0 instead of time 1, still growing by the factor 1+k each period) just multiplies the entire present value by (1+i), since the underlying cash-flow shape hasn't changed — only its position on the timeline has shifted by one period.

5.3 The Special Case k = i

Something breaks down algebraically right at k = i: the closed-form finite formula above has (i−k) in the denominator, which is zero exactly when k=i — a division by zero. But the underlying sum is still perfectly well defined; we just can't use that particular closed form to evaluate it. Go back to the term-by-term expression: when k = i, the ratio r = (1+k)/(1+i) = 1, so every term in the sum PVt = v × rt−1= v × 1t−1 = v is identical, regardless of t. Summing n identical terms of v gives:

PV, Geometric Annuity, Special Case k = iPV = n × v = n/(1+i)

In words: when the payment stream grows at exactly the discount rate, every payment's growth is exactly cancelled out by the extra period of discounting, so every payment is worth the same amount today — namely v times the first payment — and the total is just n copies of that value.

Common Trap — Dividing by Zero. If you plug numbers into the finite geometric annuity formula and get a growth rate equal (or very close) to the interest rate, do not report a division-by-zero error or an absurdly huge number as your final answer — recognize the boundary case and switch to the n × v formula instead. This special case appears on the exam specifically to test whether you understand why the formula breaks down, not just that it does.
Example 5.1 — Inflation-Adjusted Retirement Withdrawals

A retiree plans to withdraw $40,000 at the end of year 1 from an investment account, increasing the withdrawal by 3% each year (to keep pace with inflation) for 20 years. The account earns 6% effective annually. Find the present value of the full withdrawal schedule.

Show Solution

Here k = 0.03, i = 0.06, n = 20, first payment $40,000. r = 1.03/1.06 = 0.971698. Computing r20 by repeated squaring gives r20 ≈ 0.563149. Then PV factor = (1 − 0.563149)/(0.06−0.03) = 0.436851/0.03 = 14.5617. Present value: 40,000 × 14.5617 ≈ $582,468.

Example 5.2 — Growing Perpetuity Endowment

A charitable endowment is designed to pay out $20,000 at the end of year 1, with the payout growing by 2% every year thereafter, forever. The endowment earns 7% effective annually. Find the required initial endowment.

Show Solution

Since k = 2% < i = 7%, the perpetuity converges. PV = 20,000/(0.07 − 0.02) = 20,000/0.05 = $400,000.

Example 5.3 — The Boundary Case k = i

A trust pays $15,000 at the end of year 1, with each subsequent payment growing at exactly 5% per year — which happens to equal the trust's 5% effective annual earnings rate — for 12 years. Find the present value.

Show Solution

Since k = i = 5%, use the boundary formula: PV = n × (first payment) × v = 12 × 15,000 × (1/1.05) = 180,000/1.05 = $171,428.57.

Example 5.4 — Declining Subsidy Payments

A government green-energy subsidy program pays a business $8,000 at the end of year 1, with the payment declining by 4% every year (as the subsidy phases out) for 6 years. At 5% effective annual interest, find the present value of the total subsidy received.

Show Solution

Here k = −4%, i = 5%, n = 6. r = 0.96/1.05 = 0.914286, and r6 ≈ 0.584106. PV factor = (1 − 0.584106)/(0.05 − (−0.04)) = 0.415894/0.09 = 4.62105. Present value: 8,000 × 4.62105 = $36,968.36.

6. Other Non-Level Cash Flows

Learning Outcomes Covered: Value irregular cash-flow schedules that don't fit any single standard annuity pattern, using first-principles discounting and by decomposing a complex schedule into a sum of the standard annuity types already covered.

6.1 When Nothing Standard Quite Fits

Every formula in this part is ultimately a shortcut for one universal idea: the value of any cash-flow stream, at any date, is the sum of every individual payment discounted (or accumulated) to that date. When a payment schedule doesn't match a level, arithmetic, or geometric pattern, fall back on that universal equation of value directly:

General Equation of ValuePV = Σ CFt × vt (sum over every payment date t)

This always works, though it can be tedious for long schedules. The more efficient exam technique — and the one the syllabus specifically expects — is to look for a way to decompose an irregular stream into a combination of two or more of the standard building blocks from Chapters 1, 4, and 5, value each piece separately with its closed-form formula, and add the pieces together. This is exactly the same idea used in Example 4.4, where a general arithmetic annuity was split into a level piece and a pure increasing-annuity piece.

Decomposition Strategy. Faced with an irregular cash-flow diagram, ask: "Can I write this as (a level annuity) plus (an increasing or decreasing annuity, possibly deferred)?" or "Is there a lump sum sitting on top of an otherwise-standard annuity?" Almost every exam-style irregular cash flow is deliberately built from two or three standard pieces layered together — the skill being tested is recognizing the layers, not inventing a new formula.

Two habits make decomposition problems much less error-prone. First, always sketch the full cash-flow timeline before doing any algebra — irregular schedules are exactly where mental bookkeeping breaks down, and a two-second sketch prevents most of the errors. Second, when a non-level piece of the schedule doesn't begin at time 1, remember that the closed-form (Ia), (Da), or geometric-annuity formulas always value the irregular piece as of one period before its own first payment — you then need one extra discounting step to bring that value back to the problem's actual valuation date, exactly as shown in Example 6.2 below. Forgetting that extra discounting step is the most common mistake in this chapter, even among students who have the decomposition itself exactly right.

Example 6.1 — Fully Irregular Cash Flows

A settlement agreement requires payments of $1,000 at the end of year 1, $3,000 at the end of year 2, $2,500 at the end of year 3, and $4,000 at the end of year 4. At 5% effective annual interest, find the present value.

Show Solution

No standard pattern applies, so discount each payment individually. v = 1/1.05 = 0.952381. PV = 1,000(0.952381) + 3,000(0.907029) + 2,500(0.863838) + 4,000(0.822702) = 952.38 + 2,721.09 + 2,159.60 + 3,290.81 = $9,123.88.

Example 6.2 — Level Annuity Plus a Deferred Increasing Annuity

A pension pays $2,000 at the end of each of years 1 through 5, then the payments increase: $2,200 at the end of year 6, $2,400 at year 7, $2,600 at year 8, $2,800 at year 9, and $3,000 at year 10. At 5% effective annual interest, find the present value.

Show Solution

Decompose the stream into two layers: (1) a level annuity-immediate of $2,000 for all 10 years, and (2) an extra amount on top during years 6–10 of $200, $400, $600, $800, $1,000 — which is exactly 200 × (Ia)5⌐, but shifted so its first "extra" payment lands at year 6 instead of year 1. Since the increasing annuity's own internal clock starts at year 6, we value (Ia)5⌐ as of time 5 and then discount that value back 5 more years with v5.

Layer 1: 2,000 × a10⌐ = 2,000 × 7.721735 = 15,443.47.

Layer 2: at 5%, v5 = 0.783526 and (Ia)5⌐ = 12.566393. So layer 2 = 200 × 0.783526 × 12.566393 = 200 × 9.846098 = 1,969.22.

Total present value: 15,443.47 + 1,969.22 = $17,412.69. (As a check, summing all ten payments individually discounted gives the same answer to the penny.)

Example 6.3 — Annuity Plus a Balloon Payment

A loan is structured with payments of $5,000 at the end of each of 4 years, plus an additional lump-sum "balloon" payment of $20,000 due together with the final payment at the end of year 4. At 6% effective annual interest, find the present value of the total obligation.

Show Solution

Decompose into a 4-year level annuity plus a single lump sum at time 4. a4⌐ = 3.465106 and v4 = 0.792094 at 6%. PV = 5,000(3.465106) + 20,000(0.792094) = 17,325.53 + 15,841.87 = $33,167.40.

Example 6.4 — Solving for an Unknown Balloon Payment

A borrower receives $25,000 today and agrees to repay $6,000 at the end of each of years 1, 2, and 3, plus a final payment X at the end of year 4 that clears the loan entirely. At 8% effective annual interest, find X.

Show Solution

Set the loan amount equal to the present value of everything repaid. a3⌐ = 2.577097 at 8%. The known payments are worth 6,000 × 2.577097 = 15,462.58 today, leaving 25,000 − 15,462.58 = 9,537.42 in present value still to be repaid by X alone. Since X is paid at time 4, its present value is X × v4, so X = 9,537.42 × (1.08)4 = 9,537.42 × 1.360489 = $12,975.55.

The mid-stream "current value" idea from Chapter 1 (Example 1.6) extends without modification to every non-level cash flow in this part. To value an arithmetic, geometric, or fully irregular stream as of some date in the middle of its payments, split the stream at that date exactly as before: accumulate everything already paid up to the valuation date to that date, find the present value (as of that same date) of everything still to come — using whichever closed form from Chapters 4–6 matches the remaining payments' pattern — and add the two results. The remaining payments don't need to keep the same P, Q, or k that described the original schedule from time 0; you are simply re-applying the appropriate formula to whatever sub-stream is left, now treating the valuation date as a fresh time-zero.

6.2 Solving for an Unknown Quantity, in General

Every worked example in this part has fit one of two shapes: given the payment, rate, and term, find the present or future value (a direct substitution); or given the value along with all but one of {payment, rate, term}, solve for the missing piece. The second shape is where most of the genuine difficulty on the exam lives, so it is worth stating the general strategy explicitly, since it is identical across every annuity type in this part:

  1. Identify the annuity type (level, arithmetic, geometric, m-thly, continuous, or a decomposed combination) and its timing convention (immediate or due).
  2. Write the single equation of value that connects the known quantities to the unknown one, using the matching closed-form formula from this part.
  3. If the unknown is the payment amount, an increment, or a lump sum, the equation is linear in that unknown — isolate it directly, exactly as in Examples 1.2, 4.5, and 6.4.
  4. If the unknown is the number of payments n, solve the continuous (real-number) version of the equation first, then round to the nearest sensible integer number of payments and check both neighboring integers against the target, exactly as in Example 1.3 — the payment dates are inherently discrete even though the algebra treats n as continuous along the way.
  5. If the unknown is the interest rate i (or a related nominal or force-of-interest rate), there is generally no closed-form algebraic solution. Bracket the target between two trial rates and use linear interpolation, exactly as in Example 1.5, or use a financial calculator's iterative solver if one is available.

This five-step process works whether the underlying cash flow is a plain level annuity from Chapter 1 or a multi-layered combination from this chapter — the closed-form formula changes, but the solving strategy does not.

Part III — Loans (15–25%)

A loan is really just an annuity viewed from the other side of the desk: the lender hands over a lump sum today (the principal) in exchange for a promised stream of future payments whose present value, at the loan's interest rate, exactly equals that lump sum. Every question in this part of the exam is a variation on that single idea. You will be asked to find a missing piece of the puzzle — the payment, the rate, the term, or the principal — given the other pieces; to find how much of the original debt is still outstanding at some point in the middle of the loan; to split an individual payment into its interest and principal pieces; to compare two competing repayment structures (amortization versus a sinking fund); and to handle the real-world messiness of refinancing, lump-sum prepayments, and final payments that don't quite match the regular installment. None of this requires new mathematics beyond the annuity toolkit you already have — it requires knowing which tool to reach for, and being comfortable moving fluidly between the prospective (forward-looking) and retrospective (backward-looking) points of view.

Loans problems also reward patience with bookkeeping more than they reward cleverness. A borrower's monthly car payment, a homeowner's mortgage, a company's bond sinking fund, and a friend's informal IOU are all, mathematically, the same object wearing different clothes — a principal, a rate, a term, and a schedule of cash flows tying them together. Once you can move confidently between "how much is still owed," "how much of this payment was interest," and "what happens if the deal changes partway through," the rest is arithmetic. This part builds that fluency from the ground up, with fully transparent worked examples, complete schedules built row by row, and independently verified numbers at every step, so you can see exactly where each figure comes from rather than taking it on faith.

1. Loan Basics & the Amortization Method

Learning Outcomes Covered: Define and use the core vocabulary of a loan — principal, interest rate, term, level payment, outstanding balance, amortization, and the final "drop" or "balloon" payment; recognize that the original loan principal is the present value of the payment stream; construct a complete amortization schedule showing how each payment splits into interest and principal; solve for any one of principal, payment amount, interest rate, or number of payments given the other three.

1.1 The Core Idea: Principal Is a Present Value

When a lender advances an amount L and the borrower agrees to repay it with n payments of R at the end of each period, at interest rate i per period, the loan is fair (has zero economic profit built in, aside from the interest itself) precisely when the principal equals the present value of the payment stream. For a level-payment loan this gives the single most important equation of this whole topic:

Loan Principal / Level PaymentL = R · an|i  ⟺  R = L / an|i

where an|i = (1 − vn)/i is the ordinary annuity factor. Everything else in this chapter — schedules, outstanding balances, interest/principal splits — flows from treating the loan as nothing more than an annuity-immediate with present value L.

Four quantities sit inside this relationship: the principal L, the payment R, the rate i, and the number of payments n (the payment frequency is really just part of how i and n are defined — e.g. monthly payments pair with a monthly rate and a count of months). Give any three and the fourth follows: solving for L or R is direct algebra; solving for n requires logarithms and often yields a non-integer, which is exactly where the "drop payment" idea below comes from; solving for i generally has no closed form and is done by trial-and-error / interpolation (you'll practice that in the problem set).

Common trap: the final payment on a loan is almost never exactly equal to the level payment R you solved for. R is typically rounded to the nearest cent, and if the number of payments n doesn't come out to a whole number, the borrower makes n−1 full payments of the rounded R and then a smaller (or occasionally larger) drop or balloon payment at the end that exactly clears the remaining balance. Never assume the last row of a schedule automatically equals every other row.

1.2 Building an Amortization Schedule

An amortization schedule is just the loan's story told one payment at a time. For each payment date t it records: the payment made, how much of that payment covered interest on the still-owed balance, how much reduced the principal, and what the new outstanding balance is afterward. The governing recursion, applied every single period, is:

One Period of AmortizationInterestt = i · Bt−1    Principalt = R − Interestt    Bt = Bt−1 − Principalt

where Bt denotes the outstanding balance immediately after the t-th payment (and B0 = L). Chapter 3 will show a much faster shortcut for finding one row deep into a long schedule, but for now the point is to see the mechanics completely exposed.

1.3 Payment Frequency and the Interest Conversion Period

A quietly important habit to build now, before it costs you points later: the rate i and the count n that go into an|i must always describe the same period length as the payments themselves. A loan quoted as "nominal 8% compounded monthly, repaid over 5 years with monthly payments" needs i = 0.08/12 and n = 60 — not the annual rate paired with a count of years. If a problem instead gives you an annual effective rate but monthly payments, your first job is to convert that annual effective rate into the equivalent monthly rate before touching the annuity formula at all. Skipping this conversion, or converting in the wrong direction, is one of the single most common sources of wrong answers on loan problems, precisely because the arithmetic afterward all looks perfectly reasonable — it's just built on a mismatched rate.

It's also worth internalizing what "principal" really means in this context: it is not necessarily the sticker price of whatever was purchased. If a car costs $20,000 and the buyer makes a $2,000 down payment, the loan principal is $18,000 — the down payment never appears anywhere in the loan's own amortization math, because it was paid outside the loan entirely. Loan problems will sometimes bury a down payment, a trade-in credit, or a rebate in the setup precisely to test whether you correctly isolate the amount actually being financed before applying L = R·an|i.

2. Outstanding Balance

Learning Outcomes Covered: Compute the outstanding loan balance at any point during the term using the prospective method (present value of remaining payments) and the retrospective method (accumulated value of the principal minus accumulated value of payments already made); understand why the two methods must always produce the same answer; choose the more efficient method based on what information is given.

2.1 Two Vantage Points, One Balance

At any payment date t, the amount still owed on the loan — the outstanding balance Bt — can be computed by looking either forward or backward in time.

Prospective Method (look forward)Bt = R · an−t|i
Retrospective Method (look backward)Bt = L(1+i)t − R · st|i

The prospective formula says the balance is the present value of everything still to be paid. The retrospective formula says the balance is what the original loan would have grown to at compound interest, minus what the accumulated value of all payments made so far has clawed back. Both are computing the exact same number; algebraically they must agree, since both are equivalent to saying "the present value, at the loan's own rate, of the entire original payment stream splits cleanly into a part already paid and a part still owed."

Which method to use: use prospective when the future payments are cleanly known (a fixed level payment for a known remaining term) — you don't need to know anything about the loan's history. Use retrospective when the original principal and the payments actually made are cleanly known but the remaining number of payments is awkward, unknown, or non-integer (as happens whenever the original solve for n didn't come out even). Picking the wrong one doesn't give a wrong answer, just a harder path to it.
Common trap: mixing up t across the two formulas. In the prospective formula, n − t is the number of payments remaining; in the retrospective formula, t is the number of payments already made, and the accumulation factor (1+i)t applies to the original principal, not to some intermediate balance. Keep straight whether you are counting payments made or payments remaining before you plug into either formula.

2.2 Why the Two Methods Must Agree

It's worth seeing, at least once, why prospective and retrospective can't help but land on the same number — it makes the whole idea feel like a theorem rather than a coincidence. Start from the defining equation of the loan, L = R·an|i, and split the annuity on the right-hand side into its first t payments and its last n − t payments: an|i = at|i + vt·an−t|i (the first piece is the annuity of the payments already made, the second piece is the present value, as of today, of the remaining payments, discounted back over the first t periods). Multiplying the whole equation through by (1+i)t and rearranging gives L(1+i)t − R·st|i = R·an−t|i — the retrospective formula on the left, the prospective formula on the right, joined by an equals sign that came directly out of the original loan equation. They are not two different facts about the loan; they are the same fact, algebraically rearranged. That's also why either one can be used as a definition of Bt — the outstanding balance is simply whichever side of that equation is more convenient to compute given what you know.

3. Interest & Principal Portions of a Payment

Learning Outcomes Covered: Split any single payment into its interest and principal components without building the entire schedule; derive and apply the fact that, for a level-payment loan, the sequence of principal portions grows geometrically at rate (1+i); use this shortcut to find the interest or principal repaid in a payment deep into a long loan.

3.1 The Split, and Why Principal Portions Grow Geometrically

Every payment does exactly two jobs: it pays the interest that accrued on the balance since the last payment, and whatever is left over reduces the principal.

Interest / Principal SplitIt = i · Bt−1     Pt = R − It

That much is just the recursion from Chapter 1. The more useful fact — the one that lets you skip building the schedule entirely — comes from combining it with the prospective balance formula. Since Bt−1 = R·an−t+1|i, the interest portion is It = i·R·an−t+1|i = R(1 − vn−t+1), so the principal portion is:

Principal Portion, Closed FormPt = R − It = R · vn−t+1

Comparing two consecutive payments, Pt+1/Pt = vn−t/vn−t+1 = 1/v = (1+i). In words: the principal portions of a level-payment loan form a geometric sequence with common ratio (1+i). Each period, slightly more of the level payment goes to principal than the period before, growing at exactly the loan's own interest rate. Equivalently:

Geometric Growth of Principal PortionsPt = P1 · (1+i)t−1

This single fact is what lets you answer "how much principal was repaid in the 20th payment of a 30-year loan" in two lines, instead of thirty rows of arithmetic.

Common trap (sign errors): It is computed from the balance before payment t (i.e. Bt−1), never the balance after. And remember Pt = R − It, not the other way around — a surprising number of errors come from swapping which piece is "the leftover." As a running sanity check, principal portions should always increase over time (for a standard level-payment amortized loan) while interest portions decrease; if your numbers move the wrong way, you've mixed something up.

3.2 Total Interest Paid Over the Life of a Loan

A related question that shows up often: how much interest, in total, does the borrower pay over the entire life of the loan? You never need to sum n separate interest rows to answer this. Since every payment is exactly R, the total of all payments is simply nR, and the total of all principal portions must equal the original loan amount L (every dollar of principal gets repaid exactly once, by definition of a fully amortized loan). Total interest paid is just whatever's left over:

Total Interest PaidTotal Interest = nR − L

This is a useful two-second check to run against any completed schedule — in Example 1.2, for instance, 5(2{,}637.97) − 10{,}000 = 3{,}189.85, matching (up to the usual penny of rounding) the $3,189.88 total shown in that schedule's interest column. The same idea lets you answer "how much interest will be paid over just the first k payments" by combining it with the balance formulas from Chapter 2: total interest through payment k equals total payments made, kR, minus total principal repaid, which is L − Bk.

4. Sinking Fund Loans

Learning Outcomes Covered: Describe the sinking fund method as an alternative to amortization, in which the borrower pays interest only each period and separately accumulates a fund that repays the principal in one shot at the end of the term; compare the borrower's total periodic outlay under amortization versus a sinking fund arrangement; compute the effective annual rate of interest a borrower actually pays when the sinking fund earns a different rate than the loan itself charges.

4.1 Interest-Only Payments Plus a Side Fund

Amortization isn't the only way to structure loan repayment. Under a sinking fund arrangement, the borrower never reduces the loan principal directly. Instead, each period the borrower pays the lender interest only, on the full original principal L, and separately deposits a level amount into a side investment account (the "sinking fund") that earns its own rate j, which may or may not equal the loan's rate i. The deposits are sized so that, by accumulated value, the fund grows to exactly L at the end of the term — at which point the borrower withdraws the fund and pays off the loan principal in a single lump sum.

Sinking Fund DepositD = L / sn|j
Total Periodic Outlay to the BorrowerX = i·L + D

Notice the interest payment here, i·L, is constant every period — unlike amortization, where the interest portion shrinks over time because the balance shrinks. Under a sinking fund, the loan balance the borrower owes the lender never changes; what changes is the size of the side fund. It is common to also track a "net balance" equal to L minus the sinking fund's accumulated value, which does decline toward zero, but not at the same rate an amortized balance would.

4.2 Comparing the Total Cost to Amortization

If the sinking fund happens to earn exactly the loan's own rate (j = i), it is a nice algebraic fact that the total periodic outlay under the sinking fund method exactly equals the level amortization payment — the two repayment structures are financially identical, just organized differently on paper. If the sinking fund earns less than the loan rate (j < i), the sinking fund method costs the borrower strictly more per period than straight amortization; if the sinking fund earns more (j > i), it costs strictly less. This comparison is the heart of most sinking-fund exam questions.

Effective rate under a sinking fund: when j ≠ i, the borrower is effectively paying some rate i′ ≠ i on an ordinary amortized basis. To find it, compute the actual total periodic outlay X, then solve an|i′ = L/X for i′ — usually by trial-and-error/interpolation between two trial rates, exactly as you would solve for an unknown interest rate on any annuity.

4.3 Why Would Anyone Use a Sinking Fund?

Sinking funds are less common for ordinary consumer borrowing today than they once were, but they still show up regularly in corporate and municipal bond financing, where an issuer promises periodic interest ("coupon") payments to bondholders and separately builds up a fund to redeem the bond's face value at maturity, rather than amortizing the principal directly into the bondholders' hands each period. There are practical reasons a borrower might prefer this structure even when it isn't the cheapest option on paper: it can be administratively simpler to pay a level amount toward a side investment account than to recompute a declining principal schedule; it can let the borrower control where the sinking fund assets are invested, potentially capturing a higher return than the lender would ever offer as a rate concession; and historically, it let a borrower keep a large, visible principal balance on the books for tax or covenant reasons instead of showing it steadily shrink.

From the exam's point of view, the sinking fund topic is really testing whether you can keep two completely separate compound-interest calculations — the loan side, growing (or rather, sitting flat) at rate i, and the fund side, growing at rate j — from bleeding into each other. A frequent error is discounting or accumulating the sinking fund deposits at the loan's rate i instead of the fund's own rate j, or vice versa; keep firmly in mind that i only ever touches the interest-only payment and j only ever touches the sinking fund deposit and its accumulation.

5. Refinancing & Irregular Payments

Learning Outcomes Covered: Recompute a new level payment after a loan is refinanced at a new interest rate and/or a new remaining term; determine the size of a final drop or balloon payment that differs from the regular installment; combine an outstanding-balance calculation with a fresh amortization to fully solve refinancing problems; handle lump-sum prepayments and other irregular cash flows within an otherwise level-payment loan.

5.1 Refinancing Is Just "New Balance, New Annuity"

Refinancing problems look intimidating mostly because they bundle together skills from every earlier chapter of this part. Strip away the story and the recipe is always the same two steps:

Step 1 — Find the Balance Being RefinancedB = (outstanding balance at the moment of refinancing, by either method of Chapter 2)
Step 2 — Re-Amortize It as a Brand New LoanRnew = B / am|inew

where m is however many payments the new agreement calls for (which may be the same as the old remaining term, longer, or shorter) and inew is whatever rate the refinance carries (which may or may not differ from the original rate). Everything about the old loan before the refinancing date is irrelevant once B is known — the new loan is a completely fresh annuity with its own principal, rate, and term.

5.2 The Drop (Balloon) Payment

As flagged back in Chapter 1, solving an|i = L/R for n rarely produces a whole number. The standard convention is: make ⌊n⌋ full payments of the level amount, then make one more, smaller (occasionally larger) payment that exactly retires whatever balance is left. That final payment is found by taking the outstanding balance right after the last full payment and rolling it forward one more period of interest — it is not found by just rounding n up or down and hoping.

Drop / Balloon PaymentFinal Payment = B⌊n⌋ × (1+i)

5.3 Real-World Wrinkles in Refinancing

The clean two-step recipe above — find the balance, re-amortize it — captures everything the exam will ask of you, but it's worth knowing what it deliberately leaves out. Real-world refinancing usually comes with transaction costs: closing costs, appraisal fees, an origination fee, or "points" paid up front to buy down the new rate. None of these change the mechanics of the amortization math itself, but they do change whether refinancing is actually a good idea — a borrower who refinances a low remaining balance to save one percentage point of interest may find the closing costs eat up years of the resulting savings. Some loans also carry an explicit prepayment penalty for paying off (or refinancing away) the balance early, which would need to be added on top of the outstanding balance B as an extra cost at the moment of refinancing.

Exam problems will typically state plainly whether such costs exist and, if so, how to fold them in (commonly, by simply adding them to the balance being refinanced before re-amortizing, since that's cash the borrower has to come up with one way or another). The important discipline is the same one from Chapter 2: identify exactly what principal is being carried into the new loan — old balance, plus any rolled-in fees, minus any lump sum paid down — before applying the new annuity factor. Get that single number right, and the new payment is just one division away.

Part IV — Bonds (15–25%)

A bond is really just a loan wearing a certificate. An investor hands an issuer a lump sum today; in exchange the issuer promises a stream of periodic coupon payments plus a final lump-sum redemption payment. Once you notice that a bond is nothing more than an annuity (the coupons) glued to a single deferred payment (the redemption value), everything else in this part is bookkeeping — pricing, tracking how the price drifts toward par as time passes, backing out an unknown yield, and handling the wrinkle of an issuer who can pay the loan off early. This part builds the single bond-price formula from first principles in Chapter 1, then spends the rest of the part squeezing every other quantity — book value, amortization, yield, call price, term, reinvested accumulated value — out of that one equation. Expect four to six questions on the real exam drawing on this material, and expect them to reward students who keep the cash-flow diagram in their head rather than memorizing five different-looking formulas that are secretly the same equation in disguise.

1. Bond Pricing Fundamentals

Learning Outcomes Covered: Define and correctly use the vocabulary of a bond transaction — face (par) value, redemption value, coupon and coupon rate, yield rate, term, price, and the premium/discount/par relationship. Compute the price of a bond given its coupon structure, redemption value, term, and yield rate, including bonds where the redemption value differs from the face value.

1.1 The cast of characters

Every bond problem revolves around five numbers. Get comfortable naming them before you touch a formula, because exam questions love to disguise one of them or make two of them equal just to see if you notice.

  • Face value (par value), F — the number printed on the certificate. It is the amount the coupon rate is applied to; it is not automatically the amount you get back at the end.
  • Redemption value, C — the amount the issuer actually pays the bondholder at the end of the term (or at an early call, in Chapter 4). Most exam bonds are "redeemable at par," meaning C = F, but plenty of problems set C above or below F to test whether you're paying attention.
  • Coupon rate, r — a periodic interest rate applied to the face value to generate the coupon payment Fr. If coupons are paid semiannually, r is the semiannual coupon rate, not an annual one you have to halve — read the problem carefully.
  • Yield rate (yield to maturity), i — the periodic interest rate the investor actually earns on the money invested, given the price paid. This is the discount rate used to value every cash flow the bond produces.
  • Term, n — the number of coupon periods until redemption.
The single most common point-loss on bond problems has nothing to do with algebra — it's mixing up the coupon rate and the yield rate. The coupon rate tells you the size of the cash flow (Fr); the yield rate tells you how to discount those cash flows to get a price. A bond with a "high interest rate" in casual speech could mean either one, but the two play completely different roles in the formula below.

1.2 The price formula

A bond delivers two separate pieces of cash to its owner: a level annuity of coupons of size Fr for n periods, and a single lump sum of C paid out at the end of period n. Since price is nothing more than the present value of future cash flows at the yield rate, we discount each piece separately and add:

Bond Price FormulaP = Fr·an⌐i + C·vn

where v = 1/(1+i) and an⌐i = (1 − vn)/i is the ordinary annuity present-value factor. Nothing new is happening mathematically here — this is exactly the annuity and single-payment present value work from earlier parts of the syllabus, just with the two pieces given bond-specific names.

1.3 Premium, discount, and par — three flavors of the same formula

It is worth rewriting the price formula in a second, algebraically equivalent form, because this second form is what makes premium/discount reasoning transparent. Starting from P = Fr·an⌐i + C·vn, substitute an⌐i = (1 − vn)/i and simplify:

Base Amount (Premium/Discount) FormulaP = C + (Fr − Ci)·an⌐i

Look closely at the term (Fr − Ci). Fr is the actual coupon paid each period; Ci is the coupon that would be paid if the coupon rate happened to equal the yield rate applied to the redemption value. The sign of (Fr − Ci) tells you everything:

  • If Fr > Ci (equivalently, if the coupon rate is "too generous" relative to the yield), then P > C — the bond sells at a premium. Investors pay extra up front to capture those above-market coupons.
  • If Fr < Ci, then P < C — the bond sells at a discount. The coupons alone aren't attractive enough, so the market compensates by charging less than C today.
  • If Fr = Ci, then P = C exactly — the bond sells at par.

When C = F (redemption at par, by far the most common case), the comparison collapses to something you can do by inspection: compare the coupon rate r directly to the yield rate i. r > i gives a premium, r < i gives a discount, r = i gives par. This mental shortcut will save you time on the exam, but remember it's only exactly valid when C = F — when redemption value and face value differ, you must compare Fr to Ci, not r to i.

Do not compare the coupon rate r to the yield rate i when C ≠ F. The correct comparison is always between the actual coupon Fr and the "par-equivalent" coupon Ci. It is easy to get lulled into the r-vs-i shortcut and apply it blindly even when the problem has set a call price or redemption value away from face value.
Example 1.1

Granton Utilities issues a 10-year bond with face value $10,000, redeemable at par, paying annual coupons at a rate of 6%. An investor buys the bond to yield 8% annual effective. Find the price.

Show Solution

Here F = C = $10,000, so Fr = 10,000(0.06) = $600 per year, n = 10, and i = 0.08. Since the coupon rate (6%) is below the yield rate (8%), we should expect a discount, i.e., P < 10,000.

First find the annuity factor: a10⌐0.08 = (1 − 1.08−10)/0.08. Since 1.0810 = 2.158925, we get v10 = 1/2.158925 = 0.463193, so a10⌐0.08 = (1 − 0.463193)/0.08 = 6.710081.

Then P = 600(6.710081) + 10,000(0.463193) = 4,026.05 + 4,631.93 = $8,657.98. As predicted, this is comfortably below the $10,000 redemption value — a discount bond.

2. Amortization of Premium & Accumulation of Discount

Learning Outcomes Covered: Understand and compute book value at any coupon date; understand how a bond premium is written down (amortized) toward par over the life of the bond and how a discount is written up (accumulated) toward par; construct and interpret a full bond amortization/accumulation schedule.

2.1 Book value and the recursion that drives it

The book value of a bond at any point in time is simply the price the bond "should" be trading at if the original yield rate still applies — it is the present value, at the original yield i, of all remaining cash flows. Immediately after purchase, book value equals the price P. Immediately after the final coupon and redemption, book value equals C (there is nothing left to discount). In between, book value moves from P to C along a predictable path.

The cleanest way to see how book value evolves is to think of the investor as running a private savings account with the bond issuer. Each period, the outstanding balance B grows with interest at rate i, and then a coupon of Fr is withdrawn:

Book Value RecursionBt = Bt−1(1 + i) − Fr

with B0 = P (the purchase price) and, if the bond runs its full term, Bn = C automatically falls out of the recursion. You can also jump directly to any book value without stepping through the recursion period by period, using the same price formula applied to the remaining cash flows:

Direct Book Value FormulaBt = Fr·a(n−t)⌐i + C·vn−t

2.2 Where the coupon money actually goes: interest earned vs. write-down

Split each coupon payment Fr conceptually into two pieces: the portion that represents the investor's actual interest earnings that period, i·Bt−1, and whatever is left over. For a premium bond, the coupon is larger than the "fair" interest on the current book value, so the leftover piece gets subtracted from book value — this is the premium being amortized (written down):

Premium Amortized in Period tPt = Fr − i·Bt−1

For a discount bond, the coupon is smaller than fair interest, so the shortfall has to be added back onto the book value — the discount is being accumulated (written up):

Discount Accumulated in Period tDt = i·Bt−1 − Fr

Both quantities can also be written directly in terms of the base-amount quantity (Fr − Ci) from Chapter 1, discounted the appropriate number of periods:

Closed-Form Amortization / AccumulationFr − i·Bt−1 = (Fr − Ci)·vn−t+1

This closed form is extremely useful on the exam because it lets you find the amount written down (or up) in any single period — say, the 11th coupon of a 30-period bond — without ever constructing the rows before it.

Premium bonds amortize down to par; discount bonds accumulate up to par. Both book values converge to C at maturity, but they approach it from opposite directions. Mixing this up is the single most common error on amortization-schedule problems — before writing a single row, ask yourself "is book value supposed to be falling or rising here?"

From the closed form, Pt = (Fr − Ci)·vn−t+1. So P3 = (Fr − Ci)v8 = 50, and we want P8 = (Fr − Ci)v3. Taking the ratio eliminates the unknown base amount entirely:

3. Finding the Yield Rate

Learning Outcomes Covered: Given the price, coupon structure, redemption value, and term of a bond, determine the yield rate the investor is actually earning, using either a financial calculator's bond/TVM functions or, when working by hand, linear interpolation between two trial rates.

3.1 Why this is harder than the other direction

Computing price from a known yield rate is a direct plug-and-chug exercise. Going the other way — computing the yield rate from a known price — is not, because the price formula P = Fr·an⌐i + C·vn has no clean algebraic inverse in i. In practice, exam-takers handle this in one of two ways.

Financial calculator. Most calculators used for this exam (e.g., the BA II Plus) have dedicated TVM keys: enter N (number of coupon periods), PV (negative of the price, since it's a cash outflow), PMT (the coupon Fr), and FV (the redemption value C, entered separately from PV since it's a distinct cash flow), then solve for I/Y. This is fast, exact, and is how you should solve these problems when a calculator is available.

Linear interpolation. When no calculator is available (or as a way to sanity-check a calculator answer), pick two trial yield rates i1 and i2 that bracket the true yield, compute the price at each, and interpolate linearly between them:

Linear Interpolation for Yield Ratei ≈ i1 + [P(i1) − P] / [P(i1) − P(i2)] · (i2 − i1)

This works because price is a decreasing, smoothly curving (convex) function of yield — over a narrow enough bracket, that curve looks almost like a straight line, so linear interpolation gets very close to the true root. It is only an approximation, not the exact answer, but a well-chosen 1-percentage-point bracket typically gets you within a few hundredths of a percent of the true yield, which is generally close enough for exam purposes.

A good pair of trial rates almost always includes the coupon rate itself, because you already know the price at i = r without computing anything: it's exactly C. Bracket the true yield using the coupon rate as one endpoint and a nearby round number as the other, compute the actual price at just the second endpoint, and interpolate.
Example 3.1

A $1,000 bond redeemable at par pays annual coupons of 7% for 8 years and is currently priced at $945.00. Estimate the yield rate by linear interpolation.

Show Solution

At i = 7% (the coupon rate), the bond is exactly at par: P(7%) = $1,000.00. Since the actual price ($945) is below this, the true yield must exceed 7%. Try i = 8%: 1.088 = 1.850930, v8 = 0.540269, a8⌐0.08 = 5.746639. P(8%) = 70(5.746639) + 1,000(0.540269) = 402.26 + 540.27 = 942.53.

The target price 945 sits between P(8%) = 942.53 and P(7%) = 1,000.00, close to the 8% end. Interpolating:

i ≈ 7% + [1,000 − 945] / [1,000 − 942.53] × (8% − 7%) = 7% + (55/57.47)(1%) ≈ 7.96%

So the estimated yield is about 7.96%. (A financial calculator solving this exactly returns approximately 7.95% — interpolation over a 1-point bracket got us within a hundredth of a percent, which is the level of accuracy expected when working by hand.)

4. Callable Bonds

Learning Outcomes Covered: Define call price, call premium, and the mechanics of a callable bond; understand why the option to call early belongs to the issuer, not the investor; determine the price to pay for a callable bond so that a specified minimum yield is guaranteed no matter which permitted date the issuer chooses to call.

4.1 The issuer holds the option, not you

A callable bond gives the issuer — not the bondholder — the right to redeem the bond early, on one or more dates specified in advance, at a pre-set call price. The difference between the call price and the underlying par/redemption value is the call premium the issuer must pay for the privilege of retiring the debt ahead of schedule.

Because the option belongs to the issuer, the issuer will exercise it exactly when doing so benefits the issuer — which is to say, when it hurts the investor. An issuer paying an above-market coupon rate would love to call the bond back, refinance at today's lower rates, and stop overpaying; an issuer paying a below-market coupon rate has no reason to call early, since the bond is already cheap debt for them. This asymmetry is the entire reason callable-bond pricing needs its own set of rules.

Never assume the investor gets to choose when the bond is called. On the exam, "callable" always means the issuer decides. The investor's only defense is to price the bond conservatively enough, at purchase, that no matter which call date the issuer ultimately picks, the realized yield will be at least the minimum the investor demanded.

4.2 The worst-case pricing principle

Suppose an investor requires a minimum yield of i on a bond that could be called at any one of several future dates (including, possibly, holding to full maturity with no call at all). The standard approach: compute the price that would produce exactly yield i under each possible call scenario, then pay the smallest of those prices. Paying the smallest guarantees that under every other scenario the investor is effectively getting a better deal than break-even, so the realized yield is at least i regardless of what the issuer does.

When the call price is the same on every call date (the common case — e.g., "callable at par any time after year 10"), the pattern simplifies using the premium/discount logic from Chapter 1:

  • If the bond prices as a premium (Fr > Ci) at the required yield, the price is a rising function of the number of periods remaining, so the minimum price occurs at the earliest possible call date. Price to the earliest call.
  • If the bond prices as a discount (Fr < Ci), the price is a falling function of the number of periods remaining, so the minimum price occurs at the latest possible date — usually final maturity. Price to the latest call (or to maturity).
A quick mnemonic: premium bonds are "too good" for the issuer to keep paying, so they get called at the first opportunity — price to the earliest date. Discount bonds are cheap financing for the issuer, so they'll be left outstanding as long as possible — price to the latest date (maturity).
These shortcuts only apply when the call price is identical at every candidate date. As soon as the call price varies by date (e.g., a declining call-premium schedule, common with real corporate bonds), you cannot skip straight to "earliest" or "latest" — you must compute the price at every candidate date and take the minimum directly. Example 4.3 below walks through exactly this situation.
Example 4.1

A $10,000 bond pays annual coupons of 7% and is callable at par on any coupon date from year 15 through its maturity at year 20. An investor wants to be guaranteed a yield of at least 6% annual effective. What is the maximum price the investor should pay?

Show Solution

Since the coupon rate 7% exceeds the required yield of 6%, and C = F = 10,000 at every call date, this bond prices as a premium — so the rule says to price to the earliest call date, n = 15.

Fr − Ci = 700 − 600 = 100. a15⌐0.06: 1.0615 = 2.396558, v15 = 0.417265, a15⌐0.06 = 9.712250. P(15) = 10,000 + 100(9.712250) = $10,971.23.

To confirm this really is the minimum, compute the price to maturity as well: a20⌐0.06 = 11.469921 (1.0620 = 3.207135, v20 = 0.311805). P(20) = 10,000 + 100(11.469921) = $11,146.99. Since $10,971.23 < $11,146.99, the earliest call date does indeed give the lower (worst-case) price, as the premium rule predicted. The investor should pay no more than $10,971.23.

Same bond structure as Example 4.1 — a $10,000 bond, callable at par from year 15 to year 20 — but now the annual coupon rate is only 5%. The investor again requires a minimum yield of 6%. What price should be paid?

5. Other Bond Topics

Learning Outcomes Covered: Compute the accumulated value of a bond investment when coupons are reinvested at a rate different from the bond's own yield rate, and determine the overall (realized) yield earned under reinvestment; determine the term of a bond, or the point in time at which its book value first reaches a specified level, from partial information.

5.1 Reinvesting coupons at a different rate — the "two rates" problem

Everything in Chapters 1–3 assumed the investor holds the bond to a fixed horizon and that the internal rate of return on the whole transaction equals a single yield rate i. That is only true, though, if every coupon received along the way is itself reinvested at that same rate i. In practice an investor might not have access to reinvestment opportunities at the bond's own yield — coupons might sit in a savings account earning a lower rate, or get redeployed into something earning more. When the reinvestment rate j differs from i, the problem splits into two separate accumulation exercises:

Accumulated Value with ReinvestmentAVn = C + Fr·sn⌐j

where sn⌐j = [(1+j)n − 1]/j is the accumulated value of a level annuity at the reinvestment rate j. The redemption payment C needs no reinvestment assumption since it is received once, right at the horizon date. Once AVn is known, the investor's true overall realized annual yield i′ on the whole transaction (purchase price growing to AVn over n periods) satisfies:

Overall Realized YieldP(1 + i′)n = AVn  ⟹  i′ = (AVn/P)1/n − 1
Keep the bond's stated yield i, the reinvestment rate j, and the overall realized yield i′ mentally separate — they are three different numbers answering three different questions. i is what the price formula was built on; j is what happens to the coupon cash after it's received; i′ is what the investor actually earned in the end once both effects are combined.
Example 5.1

An investor buys a $1,000 par-redemption bond with 6% annual coupons and 10 years to maturity for $950. Each coupon, as it is received, is reinvested at 4% annual effective (rather than at the bond's own yield). Find (a) the accumulated value at the end of year 10, and (b) the overall annual effective yield actually realized by the investor.

Show Solution

(a) Coupons of $60/year accumulate at 4%: s10⌐0.04 = (1.0410 − 1)/0.04 = (1.480244 − 1)/0.04 = 12.0061. AV = 1,000 + 60(12.0061) = 1,000 + 720.37 = $1,720.37.

(b) The investor put up $950 and ended up with $1,720.37 ten years later. Solve 950(1+i′)10 = 1,720.37: (1+i′)10 = 1.810916, so 1 + i′ = 1.8109160.1 ≈ 1.06118. The overall realized yield is i′ ≈ 6.12% annual effective — noticeably lower than the roughly 6.9% yield-to-maturity this bond would have carried had every coupon been reinvested at the bond's own yield, a direct consequence of the coupons only earning 4% along the way.

5.2 Solving for term or for the timing of a target book value

Sometimes a problem hands you the price, coupon, redemption value, and yield, and asks you to back out the term n instead. Since the price formula is monotonic in n (strictly increasing for a premium bond, strictly decreasing for a discount bond, holding i fixed), there is exactly one value of n that works, and you find it by isolating an⌐i and then solving for n using logarithms — or, on the exam, simply testing integer values of n until the price matches.

Solving for Terman⌐i = (P − C)/(Fr − Ci)  ⟹  n = −ln[1 − i·an⌐i] / ln(1+i)

A closely related question asks not for the full term but for the specific coupon date at which book value first crosses some threshold — useful, for instance, if an accounting policy requires flagging the point at which a bond's carrying value drops below some regulatory floor. The direct book value formula from Chapter 2, Bt = Fr·a(n−t)⌐i + C·vn−t, is set equal to the target and solved for t the same way.

Part V — General Cash Flows, Portfolios & Asset-Liability Management (20–30%)

Up to this point cash flows were tidy: level annuities, fixed loan payments, single deposits growing at one interest rate. Real portfolios are messier — a pension fund receives contributions and pays benefits on irregular schedules, a bond desk holds securities that mature at different dates and were purchased at different yields, and an insurer must decide today how to invest premium income so that it will be there, dollar for dollar, when a claim comes due years from now. This part builds the toolkit for that messier world. You will learn two different ways to measure the rate of return actually earned by a fund with cash flowing in and out of it; how a full yield curve of spot and forward rates is used to price cash flows more accurately than a single flat rate can; how duration and convexity summarize a stream of cash flows' sensitivity to interest-rate movements in a single number; and how an asset manager can structure a portfolio — through Redington immunization, full immunization, or exact cash-flow matching — to protect a company against the risk that rates move against it. Together with annuities, this is the most heavily tested material on the exam, and the ideas here (duration, convexity, immunization) reappear constantly in later actuarial coursework on investments and enterprise risk management.

A useful way to read the five chapters that follow is as a progression from measurement to management. Chapter 1 measures the return a fund has already earned. Chapter 2 measures the price that a set of future cash flows should command today, given the whole term structure of rates rather than a single number. Chapters 3 and 4 measure how sensitive that price is to a change in rates — first with a linear (first-order) summary, duration, and then with a curvature (second-order) refinement, convexity. Chapter 5 is where measurement turns into action: once you can measure a portfolio's value and its rate sensitivity, you can deliberately construct assets that neutralize a liability's interest-rate risk. Keep that thread in mind, because exam questions frequently combine two or three of these ideas in a single multi-part problem — for instance, computing a spot-curve price in part (a), then that same cash flow's duration and convexity in part (b), then using both to check an immunization condition in part (c).

1. Yield Rates & Rate of Return

Learning Outcomes Covered: Define what is meant by a fund's yield rate or rate of return over a period in which money is flowing in and out of it; compute the money-weighted (dollar-weighted) rate of return using both the exact equation-of-value approach and the simple-interest approximation; compute the time-weighted rate of return by chaining sub-period growth factors; explain why the two measures can differ substantially and identify which one is appropriate for a given purpose.

1.1 What "Rate of Return" Means for an Active Fund

When a single deposit sits untouched for a year, the rate of return is unambiguous — it is just the effective annual interest rate credited. The moment a fund has contributions and withdrawals occurring mid-period, however, "the" rate of return stops being a single obvious number. A large deposit made right before a market rally will inflate the fund's dollar growth without reflecting any special skill on the part of whoever manages the money; a large withdrawal made right before a downturn will do the opposite. Actuaries and investment analysts use two distinct — and generally different — measures to describe performance in this setting: the dollar-weighted (money-weighted) rate of return, which measures the return actually experienced by the pool of money itself, and the time-weighted rate of return, which measures the return earned per dollar invested regardless of when money was added or removed.

1.2 Dollar-Weighted (Money-Weighted) Rate of Return

Suppose a fund begins a one-year period with balance B, ends with balance E, and receives net contributions Ct (positive for deposits, negative for withdrawals) at various times t measured as a fraction of the year. The exact dollar-weighted rate i is the solution of the equation of value that accumulates every flow to year-end at compound interest:

Exact Equation of Value (Dollar-Weighted)B(1+i) + Σ Ct(1+i)1−t = E

This equation is easy to state but often has no closed form, because i appears raised to fractional powers. A widely used shortcut — accurate as long as the interest rate is modest and cash flows aren't too extreme — replaces compound growth on each flow with simple interest for the remaining fraction of the year. Writing I = E − B − ΣCt for the total interest earned over the year, the approximation is:

Approximate Dollar-Weighted Ratei ≈ I / [ B + Σ Ct(1−t) ]

The denominator, B + ΣCt(1−t), is called the fund's exposure — it is the (simple-interest) average amount of money the fund had invested and "at risk" over the year. Money deposited late in the year contributes only a fraction of a full dollar-year of exposure, exactly as you'd expect.

1.3 Time-Weighted Rate of Return

The time-weighted rate of return strips out the distorting effect of cash-flow timing entirely. The idea: every time money is added or removed, close the books on a sub-period and measure the pure growth factor of a dollar sitting in the fund over just that stretch, using the fund's own market valuations immediately before and after each flow. If the fund is valued at times 0 = t0 < t1 < ⋯ < tn = 1, with Bk the fund value just before the flow at tk and Bk′ the value just after that flow (so Bk′ = Bk + Ctk), the time-weighted annual growth factor is the product of the sub-period factors:

Time-Weighted Rate of Return1 + iTW = ( B1/B0 ) × ( B2/B1′ ) × ⋯ × ( Bn/Bn−1′ )

Because each factor divides the value right before a flow by the value right after the previous flow, the size and direction of the cash flows themselves never enter the calculation — only the fund's percentage growth within each stretch matters. This is exactly why the time-weighted return is the standard for judging a portfolio manager: the manager typically has no control over when the plan sponsor happens to contribute or withdraw money, so it would be unfair (and easy to game) to let those timing decisions swing the manager's reported performance.

The same chaining idea extends naturally beyond a single year. If a fund's time-weighted return is computed separately for each of several years, the overall multi-year time-weighted return is just the product of the annual growth factors, and an average annual rate can be recovered by taking the appropriate root — for a fund with annual time-weighted returns of, say, 8%, −3%, and 11% over three years, the three-year growth factor is (1.08)(0.97)(1.11) ≈ 1.16284, and the geometric average annual return is (1.16284)^{1/3} − 1 ≈ 5.15% — noticeably below the simple (arithmetic) average of the three yearly figures, (8−3+11)/3 = 5.33%, because compounding a loss and a gain of the same size never fully cancels out. This gap between the geometric and arithmetic average return is itself a frequently tested idea: the geometric average is always the correct measure of what a dollar invested for the whole multi-year period actually earned per year, while the arithmetic average overstates it whenever returns are volatile from year to year.

Example 5.1

A charitable endowment begins the year with a market value of $100,000. Performance is weak for the first half of the year, and by June 30 (t = 0.5) the fund has fallen to $90,000 — at which point the board approves a new gift of $50,000, bringing the value immediately after the gift to $140,000. The market then rallies, and the fund ends the year at $154,000. Find (a) the time-weighted rate of return and (b) the exact dollar-weighted rate of return, and comment on the difference.

Show Solution

(a) Time-weighted: the two sub-period growth factors are, first half, $90,000/$100,000 = 0.90, and second half, $154,000/$140,000 = 1.10. Chaining them: 1 + iTW = 0.90 × 1.10 = 0.99, so iTW = −1.00%. Per dollar invested at the start, the fund actually lost value over the year — a 10% loss in the first half was only partially offset by a 10% gain on a smaller base in the second half.

(b) Dollar-weighted (exact): the equation of value is 100{,}000(1+i) + 50{,}000(1+i)0.5 = 154{,}000. Substituting x = (1+i)0.5 gives the quadratic 100{,}000x² + 50{,}000x − 154{,}000 = 0, i.e. 50x² + 25x − 77 = 0. The positive root is x = [−25 + √16{,}025]/100 ≈ 1.01590, so 1+i = x² ≈ 1.0320, giving iDW ≈ 3.20%.

The two answers point in opposite directions! The dollar-weighted return is positive because the $50,000 gift happened to arrive right before the rally, so a disproportionate share of the fund's dollars were exposed to the good half of the year — that flatters the money-weighted number even though, per dollar invested from day one, the fund actually lost 1% for the year. This is the classic exam trap: a large cash flow that happens to coincide with a change in market direction makes the dollar-weighted and time-weighted returns diverge sharply.

Common trap: Do not assume dollar-weighted and time-weighted returns will be "close." Whenever a sizable cash flow occurs near a turning point in performance, the two measures can differ by many percentage points, and can even have opposite signs, as in Example 5.1. Always re-read the problem to see which measure is actually being requested — the words "money-weighted" and "dollar-weighted" are synonyms, but neither is a synonym for "time-weighted."

That 9.0% figure says something true about how much the total pool of money grew, but it says nothing about manager skill — the manager didn't decide when the gift would arrive. The committee should evaluate the manager using the 4.50% time-weighted figure, not the 9.0% dollar-weighted figure.

Rule of thumb for word problems: if the question is about judging a manager's skill, or comparing performance to a benchmark independent of the fund's own cash-flow timing, use the time-weighted return. If the question is about how much money an investor or fund actually gained in dollar terms — including the effect of when they added or removed money — use the dollar-weighted return.

1.4 A Note on Solving the Exact Equation Beyond a Single Mid-Year Flow

Example 5.1 worked out cleanly because the one cash flow occurred at exactly t = 0.5, which turned the equation of value into an ordinary quadratic in x = (1+i)^{0.5}. With two or more flows at irregular fractional times, the exact equation of value generally has no algebraic closed form at all — the exponents on (1+i) don't share a common root, so there is no substitution that reduces the problem to a polynomial. In that situation, actuaries either fall back on the simple-interest approximation of §1.2 (which is exact when there is only one flow at exactly the midpoint of the period, and quite good otherwise for modest rates), or solve the equation numerically — for instance by iterating a first guess (often the approximate rate itself) through the exact equation and adjusting until both sides agree, which is precisely what a financial calculator's cash-flow solver or a spreadsheet's root-finder is doing behind the scenes. On the exam, you will only be asked to solve the exact equation by hand when the timing of the flows makes it reduce to a quadratic (or, occasionally, to a form solvable by inspection), so it's worth being fast and comfortable with the t = 0.5 case in particular.

It's also worth noting that the Society of Actuaries' Global Investment Performance Standards, and virtually every institutional performance report you will encounter in practice, requires time-weighted returns (or a close daily-valued approximation to them) precisely because dollar-weighted figures are so easily distorted by a client's own contribution and withdrawal decisions — a fund could look brilliant or dismal in dollar-weighted terms purely due to when its investors happened to move money, with the underlying investment performance unchanged. Keeping straight which measure a given exam question wants is as important as getting the arithmetic right.

2. Spot Rates, Forward Rates & Yield Curves

Learning Outcomes Covered: Define a spot rate as the yield on a zero-coupon investment maturing at a given date, and a forward rate as a rate contracted today for borrowing or lending over a future period; derive and apply the no-arbitrage relationship linking spot and forward rates; use a full term structure of spot rates to value a set of cash flows more precisely than a single flat yield would allow; recognize normal, inverted, and flat yield curve shapes.

2.1 Spot Rates

The spot rate for maturity t, denoted st, is the annual effective yield earned by a zero-coupon investment that is purchased today and pays exactly $1 at time t, with no cash flows in between. A one-year spot rate, a three-year spot rate, and a ten-year spot rate typically differ from one another — the collection of spot rates across all maturities is the term structure of interest rates, usually displayed as a yield curve. The present value of $1 due at time t, discounted at the spot rate for that specific maturity, is simply:

Zero-Coupon Discount FactorPV = 1 / (1+st)t

2.2 Forward Rates and No-Arbitrage

A forward rate is a rate agreed upon today for a loan or investment that begins at some future date and ends at a later future date. Write fa,b for the annual effective forward rate, contracted today, that applies to money invested from time a to time b. If markets are arbitrage-free, an investor must be indifferent between (i) investing directly for b years at the spot rate sb, and (ii) investing for a years at spot rate sa and then locking in the forward rate fa,b for the remaining b−a years. Setting the two accumulated values of $1 equal gives the fundamental relationship:

Spot–Forward No-Arbitrage Relationship(1+sa)a · (1+fa,b)b−a = (1+sb)b

Solving for the forward rate:

Implied Forward Ratefa,b = [ (1+sb)b / (1+sa)a ]1/(b−a) − 1

The one-year-ahead special case, ft,t+1 = (1+st+1)t+1/(1+st)t − 1, is the version most frequently tested. If this relationship failed to hold, an arbitrageur could borrow at one combination of rates and lend at the other for a riskless profit — that is precisely why the relationship must hold in a well-functioning market.

2.3 Valuing Cash Flows with a Spot-Rate Curve

Once a full spot-rate curve is available, the correct way to value any stream of known cash flows is to discount each payment at the spot rate matching its own maturity — not at a single blended yield. This is called maturity-matched valuation, and it is the theoretically correct generalization of present value once the yield curve is not flat:

PV of a Cash-Flow Stream Using the Spot CurvePV = Σ CFt / (1+st)t

2.4 Shapes of the Yield Curve

A normal (upward-sloping) curve has s1 < s2 < s3 < ⋯ — investors generally demand extra yield to tie up money for longer, and this is the most commonly observed shape. An inverted (downward-sloping) curve has short rates above long rates, and is often associated with market expectations that rates will fall in the future. A flat curve has spot rates roughly constant across maturities, in which case forward rates and spot rates roughly coincide. When the curve is upward-sloping, the implied one-year forward rates you compute will themselves be increasing and will each exceed the corresponding spot rate for that maturity — the market is "pricing in" higher future short-term rates.

Three broad theories are commonly offered for why the curve takes the shape it does, and it is worth being able to name them even though the exam's calculations don't depend on choosing between them. The expectations theory holds that today's forward rates are, on average, the market's best guess at future spot rates — an upward-sloping curve, under this view, simply reflects a market consensus that short-term rates will rise. The liquidity preference theory argues that investors generally need to be paid a premium to lock money up for longer (since long-dated bonds carry more price risk and less flexibility), so the curve tends to slope upward even when rates are expected to stay flat, because every forward rate embeds both an expectation and a term premium. The market segmentation theory takes a different tack entirely, holding that short-term and long-term markets are populated by largely different sets of institutional investors (a bank funding itself overnight versus a pension plan buying 30-year bonds to match 30-year liabilities), so each segment's rates are driven mainly by supply and demand within that segment rather than by a single unified expectation about the future path of rates. In practice, most economists believe all three effects are present to some degree, and a real-world yield curve reflects some blend of expected future rates, term premia, and segment-specific supply and demand.

3. Macaulay & Modified Duration

Learning Outcomes Covered: Define Macaulay duration as a present-value-weighted average time to receipt of a set of cash flows, and modified duration as the (negative) relative sensitivity of present value to a change in the interest rate; derive the precise relationship between Macaulay and modified duration, including the adjustment needed under non-annual compounding; compute either duration measure from a table of cash flows, and compute the duration of a portfolio from the durations of its components.

3.1 Macaulay Duration

For a set of cash flows CF1, CF2, …, CFn occurring at times t1, …, tn and valued at annual effective interest rate i, the Macaulay duration is the present-value-weighted average of the payment times — literally, on average, how many years an investor must wait to be repaid, where "on average" weights each payment date by the fraction of total present value it represents:

Macaulay DurationDMac = [ Σ t · CFt(1+i)−t ] / [ Σ CFt(1+i)−t ] = Σ t · PV(CFt) / PV

A single payment due at time t has Macaulay duration exactly t — there is only one payment date, so the "weighted average" is trivial. Spreading the same total present value across several dates lowers the duration if some payments come sooner, and raises it if the cash flows are pushed further into the future.

Two rules of thumb are worth internalizing, since they let you sanity-check a computed duration in seconds. First, for an ordinary coupon bond, higher coupon rates produce lower Macaulay duration, holding maturity fixed — more of the bond's total value is being returned early as coupon income, which pulls the present-value-weighted average payment date closer to today. A zero-coupon bond is the extreme case: with only one cash flow, its duration exactly equals its maturity, the longest possible duration for that maturity date. Second, duration is always less than or equal to the time of the final cash flow, and it strictly increases (all else equal) as cash flows are pushed further out — which is exactly the behavior you'd expect from any sensible notion of "average waiting time."

3.2 Modified Duration

Where Macaulay duration is a weighted-average time, modified duration is a direct measure of interest-rate sensitivity: it answers "by what percentage does the present value change per unit change in the interest rate?" Formally, writing PV(i) = Σ CFt(1+i)−t,

Modified Duration — DefinitionDmod = − (1/PV) · dPV/di

Modified duration also has a useful physical interpretation as an elasticity: it is (approximately) the percentage change in present value produced by a one-percentage-point change in the interest rate. A bond with modified duration 5 will lose roughly 5% of its value if rates rise by one full percentage point, and a bond with modified duration 15 will lose roughly three times as much for the same rate move — which is why duration is the standard single-number summary that traders and risk managers use to compare the interest-rate risk of very different cash-flow streams at a glance.

These two quantities are closely related, and the exam rewards knowing the relationship precisely rather than approximately. Differentiate PV(i) term by term:

dPV/di = Σ CFt · (−t)(1+i)−t−1 = − 1/(1+i) · Σ t·CFt(1+i)−t = − 1/(1+i) · DMac · PV

Substituting into the definition of Dmod gives the clean result:

Macaulay–Modified Duration Relationship (Annual Effective Rate)Dmod = DMac / (1+i)

3.3 The Nominal-Compounding Adjustment — Read This Carefully

The formula above assumes present value is computed with annual effective rate i, i.e. PV = ΣCFt(1+i)−t with t in years. If instead cash flows are discounted using a nominal annual rate i(m) compounded m times per year, so that PV = ΣCFt(1 + i(m)/m)−mt, the same differentiation (with respect to i(m)) gives:

Macaulay–Modified Duration Relationship (Nominal Rate, m-thly Compounding)Dmod = DMac / (1 + i(m)/m)

In words: you always divide Macaulay duration by one plus the periodic effective rate that matches whatever compounding convention was used to discount the cash flows in the first place — that periodic rate is i itself under annual effective compounding, but it is the smaller quantity i(m)/m under m-thly nominal compounding. Note that t (and therefore Macaulay duration) stays measured in years either way; only the divisor changes.

Common trap: Do not automatically divide Macaulay duration by (1+i) where i is a nominal annual rate. If a bond's yield is quoted as "8% convertible semiannually," the correct divisor is (1 + 0.08/2) = 1.04, not (1+0.08) = 1.08. Mixing these up is one of the single most common point-losing errors on this topic.

3.4 Duration of a Portfolio

Because present value and its derivative with respect to i are both additive across cash-flow streams, the modified duration of a portfolio made of several separately-valued holdings is simply the present-value-weighted average of the component modified durations (and the same is true for Macaulay duration, valued at a common rate):

Duration of a PortfolioDportfolio = Σ PVk · Dk / Σ PVk

4. Convexity & Approximating Change in PV

Learning Outcomes Covered: Define and compute the convexity of a set of cash flows; use modified duration to build a first-order (linear) approximation of the percentage change in present value caused by a shift in interest rates, and build the same first-order approximation starting instead from Macaulay duration; add a convexity term to build a second-order approximation; compare approximate and exactly recomputed present values to see the size and direction of the approximation error.

4.1 First-Order Approximation via Modified Duration

Modified duration was defined as Dmod = −(1/PV)(dPV/di), which is precisely the setup for a first-order (tangent-line) Taylor approximation of PV around the current rate. For a small change in rate Δi:

First-Order Approximation (via Modified Duration)ΔPV / PV ≈ − Dmod · Δi

The minus sign is the whole point of the formula: when rates rise (Δi > 0), present value falls, and vice versa. A rate increase should always produce a negative estimated ΔPV.

Common trap: Sign errors are the single most common mistake with this formula. Before finalizing an answer, ask yourself whether rates rose or fell, and check that your ΔPV has the opposite sign from Δi.

4.2 First-Order Approximation via Macaulay Duration

Because Dmod = DMac/(1+i), the same first-order approximation can be written starting from Macaulay duration instead — the formula looks different, but a correctly-applied version must produce an identical numerical answer, since the two duration measures are just rescalings of each other:

First-Order Approximation (via Macaulay Duration)ΔPV / PV ≈ − DMac · Δi / (1+i)

Students sometimes see this as a "different" or more conservative formula, but it is algebraically the same statement as the modified-duration version — simply substitute Dmod = DMac/(1+i) into the boxed formula in §4.1 and the Macaulay-duration version drops out immediately.

Common trap: Never plug Macaulay duration directly into the modified-duration formula (i.e. never compute −DMac·Δi without dividing by 1+i). This overstates the true sensitivity by a factor of (1+i) and is a frequently-tested distractor answer choice.

4.3 Convexity

The first-order approximation is a straight line tangent to the true (curved) present-value function, so it systematically misses by more as Δi grows. Convexity captures the curvature — the second derivative of present value with respect to i, again scaled by present value:

Convexity — Definitionc = (1/PV) · d²PV/di²

Differentiating dPV/di = −Σ t·CFt(1+i)−t−1 once more with respect to i gives d²PV/di² = Σ t(t+1)·CFt(1+i)−t−2, so:

Convexity — Computational Formc = [ Σ t(t+1) · PV(CFt) ] / [ PV · (1+i)² ]

(This manual consistently uses the t(t+1) convention above, obtained by differentiating PV(i) exactly twice with respect to i — make sure any formula sheet you use defines convexity the same way, since some sources use a slightly different scaling.)

The name "convexity" is a literal description: if you were to plot present value against the interest rate, the graph curves upward (is convex) rather than sitting on a straight line, because d²PV/di² > 0 for any ordinary stream of fixed positive cash flows. A first-order approximation is a straight line drawn tangent to that curve at today's rate; since the true curve bows upward and away from its tangent line in both directions, the true present value always sits at or above the straight-line estimate, whether rates rise or fall. That geometric picture is worth keeping in mind — it's a faster way to remember why the convexity adjustment in §4.4 is always added, rather than trying to re-derive the sign from the algebra under exam time pressure.

4.4 Second-Order (Convexity-Adjusted) Approximation

Adding the next term of the Taylor expansion produces a noticeably more accurate approximation, especially for larger rate shifts:

Second-Order ApproximationΔPV / PV ≈ − Dmod·Δi + (1/2)·c·Δi²

Notice the convexity term is always added, regardless of the sign of Δi (since Δi² is never negative) — this reflects the fact that a positively-convex cash-flow stream loses less value than the linear approximation predicts when rates rise, and gains more than the linear approximation predicts when rates fall. Convexity is a friend to the holder of the cash flows in both directions.

Every ordinary, option-free stream of fixed cash flows — a loan, a coupon bond, a level annuity, a single liability payment — has this favorable (positive) convexity, because the present-value function PV(i) = ΣCFt(1+i)−t is a sum of convex functions of i, and a sum of convex functions is itself convex. It is only once cash flows themselves start changing in response to the interest rate — a callable bond that the issuer can redeem early when rates fall, or a mortgage pool where homeowners refinance en masse when rates drop — that convexity can turn negative, meaning the security behaves worse than the linear approximation in both directions rather than better. Those option-embedded instruments are outside this exam's scope, but it is worth remembering that the reliably favorable convexity used throughout this chapter depends on the cash flows themselves being fixed and not contingent on the interest-rate path.

5. Cash-Flow Matching & Immunization

Learning Outcomes Covered: Define and apply Redington immunization, including all three of its required conditions; define full immunization and describe the sandwiched (bracketing) cash-flow structure that protects against interest-rate changes of any size, not merely small ones; define exact cash-flow matching (dedication) and explain how it eliminates reinvestment risk entirely; compare the three techniques on what risk each protects against and what each requires of the asset manager.

5.1 Redington Immunization

An insurer or pension plan is immunized against small, parallel shifts in the interest rate if the surplus (assets minus liabilities, in present-value terms) does not fall when rates move slightly. Redington's classical result gives three conditions on the asset and liability cash flows, all measured at the current interest rate, that together guarantee this local protection:

Redington Immunization — Three Conditions(1) PV(Assets) = PV(Liabilities) (2) Dmod(Assets) = Dmod(Liabilities) (3) c(Assets) > c(Liabilities)

Condition (1) says the plan starts fully funded. Condition (2) says the first-order sensitivities of assets and liabilities to a rate change cancel out — this is what makes the surplus flat, to a first approximation, near the current rate. Condition (3) is the one students most often forget: matching present value and duration alone only guarantees the surplus is stationary at the current rate — it says nothing about whether that stationary point is a local minimum (bad — the surplus falls whichever way rates move) or a local maximum (good). Requiring the asset cash flows to be more dispersed in time than the liability cash flows (which is what greater convexity means here) is what tips the balance in the insurer's favor.

Common trap: All three Redington conditions must hold simultaneously — a portfolio that matches present value and duration but has lower convexity than the liabilities is not immunized at all; it is actually exposed to loss from rate movements in either direction. Don't stop checking after condition (2).

5.2 Full Immunization

Redington immunization only guarantees protection against small rate changes — it is a statement about derivatives at a single point. Full immunization is a stronger result available in a special case: when the liabilities consist of a single cash flow, and the assets consist of exactly two cash flows positioned so that one falls before the liability date and the other falls after it (with present value and duration matched, exactly as in Redington's conditions 1 and 2), it can be shown that the asset portfolio's present value is at least as large as the liability's present value at every possible interest rate, not merely in a neighborhood of the current rate. This "sandwiching" of the liability cash flow between two asset cash flows is the classic full-immunization structure, and Example 5.17's portfolio (assets at t = 2 and t = 9, liability at t = 5) is in fact exactly this structure.

Example 5.19 — Cash-Flow Matching (Dedication)

A pension plan must pay $200,000 at t = 1, $250,000 at t = 2, and $300,000 at t = 3, and wants a portfolio with zero reinvestment risk — cash arrives exactly when needed, in exactly the right amount, with nothing left to reinvest at an uncertain future rate. Available bonds (face amount flexible, priced at their stated coupon and maturity): a 1-year zero-coupon note; a 2-year bond with 6% annual coupons; and a 3-year bond with 8% annual coupons. Construct the dedicated portfolio using backward induction (fund the last liability first, since the longest bond's coupons will help pay the earlier liabilities too).

Show Solution

Step 1 — fund t = 3. Buy face amount FC of the 3-year, 8% bond so its maturity payment covers the year-3 liability: FC(1.08) = 300{,}000 ⟹ FC ≈ $277{,}778. This bond also throws off coupons of 0.08 × 277{,}778 ≈ $22{,}222 at both t = 1 and t = 2.

Step 2 — fund the remainder of t = 2. After the 3-year bond's coupon, the plan still needs 250{,}000 − 22{,}222 = 227{,}778 at t = 2. Buy face amount FB of the 2-year, 6% bond: FB(1.06) = 227{,}778 ⟹ FB ≈ $214{,}885. This bond also pays a coupon of 0.06 × 214{,}885 ≈ $12{,}893 at t = 1.

Step 3 — fund the remainder of t = 1. The plan still needs 200{,}000 − 22{,}222 − 12{,}893 ≈ $164{,}885 at t = 1, exactly covered by buying $164,885 face of the 1-year zero.

Verification: at t = 1, total cash in = zero's maturity ($164,885) + B's coupon ($12,893) + C's coupon ($22,222) = $200,000 ✓. At t = 2, total cash in = B's maturity-plus-coupon ($227,778) + C's coupon ($22,222) = $250,000 ✓. At t = 3, C's maturity payment = $300,000 ✓. Every liability is funded exactly, dollar for dollar and date for date — no reinvestment of "leftover" cash is ever required.

Example 5.20 — Why the Convexity Condition Matters

An insurer's liabilities consist of $400,000 due at t = 1 and $600,000 due at t = 9, valued at i = 5% (a "barbell" liability profile, with PV ≈ $767,720, duration ≈ 5.03 years, and convexity ≈ 42.0). Rather than matching this dispersion, the asset manager buys a single bullet bond maturing at t = 5.03 with the same present value and duration. Show that this asset portfolio's convexity is lower than the liabilities' convexity, and demonstrate the consequence by recomputing the surplus after rates fall to 4%.

Show Solution

A single cash flow at t = 5.03 has convexity t(t+1)/(1+i)² = (5.03)(6.03)/1.05² ≈ 27.5, well below the liabilities' convexity of about 42.0 — Redington's third condition fails even though present value and duration are matched.

At i = 4%, recomputing: PV(Liabilities) = 400{,}000/1.04 + 600{,}000/1.04⁹ ≈ 384{,}615 + 421{,}550 ≈ $806{,}170. The single bullet asset, discounted from its original value using its own duration-implied growth, comes to roughly $805{,}580. The surplus, which was exactly zero at i = 5% by construction, has turned negative — roughly −$590 — purely because of the interest-rate move, even though PV and duration were matched at the outset. This is precisely the failure mode Redington's convexity condition is designed to prevent: with insufficient asset convexity relative to the liabilities, a "duration-matched" book can still lose money when rates move.

5.3 Comparing the Three Techniques

Redington Immunization vs. Full Immunization vs. Cash-Flow Matching
TechniqueProtects AgainstKey RequirementsMain Limitation
Redington ImmunizationSmall, one-time parallel shifts in the interest ratePV, duration matched; asset convexity > liability convexityOnly a local (small-shift) guarantee; portfolio must be rebalanced as time passes and rates move

A quick way to keep the three techniques straight: Redington gives you protection near today's rate; full immunization (a special case of Redington's structure) gives you protection at any rate, but only for a single liability date; dedication sidesteps the interest-rate question altogether by making sure you never have to reinvest anything in the first place.

5.4 Practical Limitations Worth Knowing

None of these three techniques is a "set it and forget it" solution, and it is worth understanding why in addition to knowing how to construct the portfolios themselves. Redington immunization is a statement about the current instant only: durations drift as time passes (even if rates never move, a cash flow scheduled two years from now has a duration of only one year after twelve months elapse) and convexity relationships can shift too, so an immunized portfolio must be periodically rebalanced — re-matching present value and duration — to stay immunized. Every rebalancing incurs transaction costs, and the gap between rebalancing dates exposes the plan to what practitioners call immunization risk: the residual risk that, between rebalancing dates, the yield curve does not move as a single parallel shift (the implicit assumption behind Redington's derivative-based argument) but instead twists or bends, which can erode the protection the duration and convexity match were meant to provide. Full immunization, similarly, depends on the liability truly being a single cash flow — real pension and insurance liabilities are almost always streams of payments spread across many future years, so full immunization in its cleanest textbook form is more often a useful mental model than a literal off-the-shelf solution.

Cash-flow matching avoids immunization risk entirely, since there is no reliance on duration or convexity approximations at all — the assets simply pay out exactly what is owed, when it is owed. Its cost is opportunity cost: locking in a portfolio of specific bonds to hit specific future dates is typically more expensive (in present-value terms, since it constrains the investment choice set) than a duration-matched portfolio built from whatever securities currently offer the best available yields, and suitable zero-coupon or near-zero instruments may simply not exist at every maturity a liability schedule requires. In practice, many institutions use a blend: dedicating (exactly matching) the nearest, most certain liabilities where reinvestment risk would be most costly to bear, while managing more distant or less certain liabilities with an immunization approach that can be rebalanced as circumstances change.

21. Explain, in your own words, why matching only present value and duration between assets and liabilities (Redington's first two conditions) is not by itself sufficient to guarantee that a rate change will never hurt the surplus.
Show Answer
Matching PV and duration only guarantees that the surplus function (assets minus liabilities, as a function of the interest rate) has a zero first derivative — i.e., a flat tangent line — at the current rate. That makes the current rate a stationary point of the surplus, but a stationary point can be a local maximum or a local minimum. Only the additional requirement that asset convexity exceed liability convexity guarantees the stationary point is a local maximum, so that surplus can only rise (or stay level, to first order) as rates move away from today's level in either direction — which is the actual goal of immunization.
22. Show Answer
The 9.8% dollar-weighted figure is inflated by the timing of the employer's contribution — money that happened to be added right before the rally earned a large weight in the dollar-weighted calculation purely because of when it arrived, not because of any decision the manager made.
